## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 The question every two-group test answers

    *"Is the difference I see between group A and group B larger than the difference I would expect from chance alone — and how
    big is it?"*

    Three ingredients decide the answer:

    | Ingredient | Bigger means… | Retail example |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Signal** — the observed difference | easier to detect | test stores grew more than control |
    | **Noise** — how much units vary | harder to detect | stores differ hugely in size |
    | **Sample size** — how many independent units | easier to detect | 20 stores per arm |

    A test statistic is simply **signal ÷ noise**, where noise shrinks as sample size grows.

    ### 2.2 Step 1 — what is the unit of analysis?

    **The unit of analysis is whatever was randomised (or sampled independently).** NorthStar randomised *stores*, so each store
    contributes **one** number. Treating 320 store-weeks or thousands of transaction lines as independent pretends you have far
    more information than you do — the p-value becomes too small and the interval too narrow.

    ### 2.3 Step 2 — what type is the outcome?

    | Outcome | Example | Summary per group | Test |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Continuous** | units, revenue, growth index | mean (and median) | **Welch's t-test** |
    | **Binary** | defaulted yes/no | proportion (rate) | **Two-proportion z-test** (Fisher's exact if counts are small) |

    ### 2.4 Welch's t-test (continuous outcome)

    **In plain English:** *t = (difference in group means) ÷ (standard error of that difference).* The standard error combines
    each group's spread divided by its size, so groups may have **different** variances (that is what makes it Welch's version —
    the safer default).

    \[ t = \frac{\bar x_A - \bar x_B}{\sqrt{s_A^2/n_A + s_B^2/n_B}} \]

    A large |t| means the difference is many standard errors away from zero. The p-value is the probability of a difference at
    least this large **if** the true difference were zero. The 95% **confidence interval** is roughly the difference ± 2 standard
    errors: the range of true effects compatible with the data.

    ### 2.5 Making stores comparable — growth versus their own baseline

    Hypermarkets sell many times more Hair Care than express stores. If, by chance, a few more big stores land in one arm, a
    simple comparison of weekly averages is swamped by size differences. The fix used here: for each store, divide its average in
    the test window (weeks 70–77) by **its own** average in the 16 weeks before (54–69). This **growth index** removes size; the
    test then compares the average growth index between arms. (Day 4 generalises this idea as CUPED.)

    ### 2.6 Two-proportion z-test (binary outcome)

    **In plain English:** *z = (difference in rates) ÷ (standard error of the difference under "no difference").* With large
    counts this is reliable; when any expected cell count is below about 5, use **Fisher's exact test** instead.

    ### 2.7 When the data are heavy-tailed or small

    If the outcome is very skewed (basket values!) **and** groups are small, a few extreme values can dominate the mean. Then use a
    **Mann-Whitney U test** (compares ranks: "does a random unit from A tend to exceed one from B?") or a **bootstrap CI**. With
    large samples, the t-test on means stays valid thanks to the central limit theorem — but always report the median too.

    ### 2.8 Effect size and practical significance

    | Measure | Use for | Meaning |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Difference and **% lift** with CI | continuous | how much more, in business units |
    | **Cohen's d / Hedges' g** | continuous | difference in standard deviations (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large — Cohen's rough benchmarks) |
    | **Risk difference** (pp) with CI | binary | extra events per 100 |
    | **Relative risk**, **odds ratio** | binary | ratio of rates / odds |

    **Statistically significant ≠ important.** With huge samples tiny effects become "significant"; with small samples important
    effects can be "not significant". Decide on the **confidence interval versus the smallest effect worth acting on**.

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh in five minutes"
        - Two-group test = *signal ÷ noise × √n*. Unit of analysis = unit of randomisation.
        - Continuous → **Welch t** (default; tolerates unequal variances). Binary → **two-proportion z** (Fisher if expected cells < 5).
        - Heavy tails + small n → Mann-Whitney / bootstrap. Large n → t on means is fine; still show medians.
        - Report **effect size + CI**; judge against the **minimum effect worth acting on**, not against p < 0.05.
        - Experiments: check **randomisation and sample ratio** before the result.

    **Where judgement is needed** — the five questions that decide whether a two-group result can be trusted:

    1. **What was randomised, and is that the unit analysed?** (stores vs lines vs store-weeks)
    2. **Were the groups comparable before the treatment?** (balance check, sample ratio)
    3. **Is the outcome defined identically for both groups and over the same window?**
    4. **How big is the effect, and how uncertain?** (CI against the business threshold)
    5. **What else could produce this difference?** (confounding, seasonality, mix, novelty, data errors)

    **Where practitioners genuinely disagree** (know the positions; pick one and be consistent):

    - *Welch by default vs Student's t.* Many methodologists recommend Welch always (it loses little when variances are equal).
      This course follows that view.
    - *Normality tests before t-tests.* Often discouraged: at large n they reject trivially, at small n they lack power. Look at the plot.
    - *α = 0.05.* A convention, not a law. Some propose 0.005 for new claims; regulated settings may pre-specify their own.
    - *One-sided tests.* Defensible only if pre-registered and a result in the other direction would genuinely lead to the same decision.
    - *Frequentist vs Bayesian readouts.* Bayesian "probability the promotion lifts sales by ≥ 5%" is often easier to act on (L4.3).

## 3. When to use, when not, assumptions, fallbacks

| | Continuous (Welch t) | Binary (two-proportion z) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Use when** | comparing average amounts between two independent groups | comparing a rate between two independent groups |
| **Do not use when** | units are paired (→ paired t), clustered and not aggregated (→ aggregate / mixed model), > 2 groups (→ ANOVA), groups not randomised and causal claim needed (→ causal methods) | counts tiny (→ Fisher), paired yes/no (→ McNemar), covariates matter (→ logistic regression) |
| **Assumptions** | independent units; means approximately normal (n ≥ ~30 per group *or* not heavy-tailed) | independent units; expected cells ≥ 5; same definition and window |
| **Diagnostics** | histogram / ECDF and Q-Q per group, skewness, Levene (informative only), outliers | 2×2 table with expected counts; rates by segment |
| **Fallback if a check fails** | Mann-Whitney, bootstrap CI, permutation test, log or growth transform | Fisher's exact; logistic regression; stratified (Mantel-Haenszel) analysis |
| **Recipe** | `rcp_two_group_test` (auto-detects outcome type) | same |

## 6. Scenario matrix

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    | Result pattern | Interpretation | Next check |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Significant, CI entirely above the threshold | Effect is real and large enough | Guardrails (margin, returns), then roll out |
    | Significant, CI straddles the threshold | Effect is real; may be too small to pay | Extend test or staged rollout |
    | Not significant, CI wide (includes 0 and the threshold) | **Inconclusive** — test too small | Power / MDE; bigger or longer test |
    | Not significant, CI narrow around 0 | Evidence of no meaningful effect | Stop; record the learning |
    | t-test and Mann-Whitney disagree | Tails or outliers drive the mean | Inspect extremes; report both |
    | Huge n, tiny effect, p ≈ 0 | Statistically, not practically, significant | Judge on effect size |

=== "Leader"

    | Result pattern | Interpretation | Likely causes | Next checks | Possible actions — why / when / when not | Norms (rules of thumb) |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Significant; lower CI ≥ threshold** | Real and worthwhile | Genuine effect | Guardrails; novelty (does lift decay week by week?); format/segment consistency | **Roll out** — when guardrails hold and the test resembles rollout conditions. *Not* when the test period was unusual (holiday) | α 0.05 two-sided, power 80% (conventions) |
    | **Significant; CI straddles threshold** (this retail test) | Real, size uncertain | Small test | MDE vs threshold; cost of a wrong rollout vs delay | **Staged rollout with a holdout** — when the downside is reversible. **Extend test** — when rollout is costly/irreversible. *Not* full rollout on the point estimate | Many trade promotions fail to break even in industry studies — size matters more than significance |
    | **Not significant; wide CI** | Inconclusive | Under-powered (MDE ≫ threshold) | Power calculation; variance reduction | **Redesign** (more stores, longer pre-period, CUPED). *Never* report "no effect" | Ask for MDE before launch |
    | **Not significant; narrow CI around 0** | No meaningful effect | Ineffective treatment | Implementation fidelity (did stores execute?) | **Stop**; reallocate budget | — |
    | **Effect far larger than plausible** | Too good to be true | Wrong unit, data error, confounded rollout, SRM | Row accounting; sample ratio; pre-period balance; outliers | **Hold** until explained | Feature-only promos rarely exceed ~20% (varies) |
    | **Pre-period imbalance** | Arms differed before treatment | Chance with few units; broken randomisation | Randomisation log; stratification | Use growth vs baseline / ANCOVA; re-randomise future tests | Stratify by format/region |
    | **Large overall gap, smaller within segments** (finance) | Confounding by mix | Channel, vintage, product mix | Stratify; regression with covariates | **Do not** set policy on the raw gap; estimate the adjusted effect | Always stratify by channel and vintage in credit |
    | **Tiny effect, p ≈ 0, huge n** | Not practically important | Large portfolio | Effect per 1,000; cost/benefit | Act only if the economics justify it | Translate to money |

    !!! question "Questions to ask the junior"
        - "What was randomised, and how many independent units do you have?"
        - "Show me the pre-period balance and the sample ratio."
        - "What is the CI, and what is the smallest effect that would change our decision?"
        - "What else could explain this — season, mix, a parallel campaign, a data change?"
        - "If you had found the opposite result, what would you have recommended?"

    !!! danger "Red flags in a junior's two-group analysis"
        n in the thousands for a store test · p-value without CI · "no difference" from a non-significant result · t-test on a 0/1
        outcome without proportion language · one-sided test chosen after seeing the data · segments sliced until one is significant ·
        outcome window differs between groups · post-outcome fields in the comparison.

## 11. Coaching cards and recap

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    **Recap**

    - Analyse at the unit that was randomised.
    - Continuous → Welch t; binary → two-proportion z (Fisher if small); heavy tails + small n → Mann-Whitney / bootstrap.
    - Always report effect size **with a CI**; compare it with the smallest effect worth acting on.
    - Non-significant ≠ no effect. Significant ≠ important.
    - Before believing a difference, ask what else could explain it.

=== "Leader"

    !!! tip "Coaching card 1 — interpreting the number"
        Ask the junior to say the result in one sentence **without** the word "significant". Example: *"Test stores grew about {{ a.l34_hc_lift_pct }}% more
        than control; the true lift could plausibly be anywhere from {{ a.l34_hc_ci_lo }}% to {{ a.l34_hc_ci_hi }}%."* If they cannot, they do not yet own the result.

    !!! tip "Coaching card 2 — business interpretation"
        Make them put the CI next to the business threshold on a single line chart. The decision becomes visible: roll out, stage,
        extend or stop. Then ask what the cost of each wrong decision is.

    !!! tip "Coaching card 3 — the confounding reflex"
        For any observational comparison (verification, loyalty), require a stratified table by the two most likely confounders
        before any recommendation. In credit: channel and vintage. In retail: format and region.
