**Objectives** — you can: describe the relationship between two variables with the right tool (scatter, Pearson, Spearman, Kendall,
crosstab, Cramér's V, grouped summaries); explain why a correlation can be near zero while a relationship is strong, or large while
there is no causal link; and avoid reading correlation as causation.

## 1. Business question

=== "Retail"

    > "Does price affect quantity? Does warm weather sell drinks? Do loyalty tiers buy more on promotion?"

=== "Finance"

    > "How tightly are limits tied to income — and is utilisation a weak risk driver because its correlation with default is small?"

## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 Scatter first

    Always plot before computing a coefficient. Four datasets with identical correlations can look completely different
    (Anscombe's quartet): a line, a curve, one outlier driving everything, or a cluster plus one point.

    ### 2.2 Correlation coefficients

    | Coefficient | Measures | Use | Example |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Covariance** | joint variation in original units | building block | not comparable across scales |
    | **Pearson r** | linear association (−1 to 1) | roughly linear, few outliers | income vs limit **0.96** |
    | **Spearman ρ** | monotone association of ranks | curved but monotone; outliers | income vs limit **0.898** |
    | **Kendall τ** | concordant vs discordant pairs | small n, many ties | price vs quantity -0.062 |
    | **Point-biserial** | Pearson with a binary variable | numeric vs yes/no | utilisation vs default **0.1** |

    Pearson > Spearman for income–limit: the tie is close to linear and the largest incomes have proportionally large limits.

    ### 2.3 Small correlation, strong relationship

    Utilisation's correlation with default is only 0.1, yet accounts above 90% utilisation default
    **6.129×** as often as those at or below 50%. With a rare binary outcome and a threshold-shaped effect, r is
    small by construction. **Look at binned rates**, not just r.

    ### 2.4 Correlation without causation

    Regional weekly temperature correlates with Beverages (r = 0.463) and Frozen (0.447) — but also
    with **Hair Care (r = 0.17)**, which the data contain no weather effect for. Shared drivers — seasonality,
    holidays, region size — produce correlations on their own. Price and quantity across all products correlate at only
    -0.076: expensive products (electronics) are bought one at a time, cheap ones in multiples — product type confounds
    the relationship. Elasticity needs within-product price variation (Day 8).

    ### 2.5 Two categorical variables

    A **contingency table** (crosstab) with the right percentages: share of baskets containing a promoted item by loyalty segment —
    bronze 41.7% · gold 61.6% · none 46.3% · silver 52.2%. **Cramér's V** summarises strength (0 = none, 1 = perfect): **0.128** —
    weak, though "highly significant" (p ≈ 3.62e-11) because n is large.

    ### 2.6 Grouped summaries

    Most business "relationships" are grouped summaries: median basket by format, default rate by employment type. Always show n per
    group and a spread measure.

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        Scatter first. Pearson = linear, Spearman = monotone, Kendall = small samples/ties, Cramér's V for categories. Small r does not
        mean weak risk; large r does not mean causation. Significant ≠ strong at large n.

    **Judgement points**

    - **Feature screening by |r| is dangerous.** Dropping utilisation because r = 0.1 would discard one of the
      strongest risk drivers. Ask for binned outcome rates.
    - **Spurious correlations are everywhere in time series.** Two series that both peak in Q4 correlate. Ask "what else moves both?" and
      for de-seasonalised or within-period comparisons.
    - **Very high r between predictors (income–limit 0.96) predicts multicollinearity** (Day 5). Decide upfront which
      variable carries the business meaning.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        When a junior shows a correlation, ask them to name **one confounder** and **one mechanism**. If they can name a confounder but not
        a mechanism, the correlation is a hypothesis, not a finding.

## 3. When to use which

| Variables | Tool | Fallback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two numeric, linear | scatter + Pearson | Spearman if outliers/curvature |
| Two numeric, curved monotone | Spearman | binned means |
| Numeric vs binary outcome | binned outcome rates + point-biserial | logistic regression (Day 5) |
| Two categorical | crosstab + Cramér's V | chi-square test (Day 3) |
| Over time | de-seasonalise first | difference the series (Day 7) |

## 5. Output anatomy

| Number | Meaning | Typical range | Suspicious when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| r = 0.96 (income–limit) | near-deterministic link | policy-driven fields often > 0.8 | two "independent" drivers with r > 0.9 → collinearity |
| r = 0.1 (util–default) | weak linear link with a rare outcome | binary outcomes rarely exceed 0.3 | concluding "irrelevant" |
| V = 0.128 | weak association | 0.1 weak, 0.3 moderate, 0.5 strong (rough) | tiny p with tiny V |
| Hair Care r = 0.17 | spurious via shared drivers | — | any "effect" in a control category |

## 6. Scenario matrix

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    | Pattern | Meaning | Next |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Pearson ≪ Spearman | monotone but curved, or outliers | plot; use Spearman |
    | Pearson ≫ Spearman | a few extreme points drive r | check influence |
    | Control variable also correlates | shared driver | de-seasonalise / stratify |
    | Small r, big binned differences | threshold effect or rare outcome | report binned rates |

=== "Leader"

    | Pattern | Likely causes | Action — why / when / when not | Norm |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Marketing claims weather drives sales | seasonality, holidays | **Require a control category** and de-seasonalised analysis | Placebo/control checks |
    | Junior drops low-r features | misunderstanding of r | **Coach**: binned rates, model-based importance | Never screen on r alone |
    | Two predictors r > 0.9 | policy link | Choose one or combine (ratio) before modelling | VIF check (Day 5) |

## 7. Alternatives

!!! tip "What else could I try?"
    - **[Contingency table with row/column % and Cramér's V](../../navigator/#N-DESC-XTAB)** — Two categorical variables.
    - **[Stratified analysis — check for Simpson's paradox and confounding](../../navigator/#N-DESC-STRATIFY)** — An overall comparison may be distorted by a third variable (format, channel, vintage).
    - **[Non-linear effects — bins/WoE, splines](../../navigator/#N-REG-NONLINEAR)** — Risk jumps above a threshold (utilisation > 80%).
    - **[Causal diagram (DAG) — adjust for confounders, not colliders or mediators](../../navigator/#N-CAUSAL-DAG)** — Any observational causal question.

## 9. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R1-15 · Applied · Correlations that mislead"
    **Business framing:** Two quick claims — "price barely affects quantity" and "warm weather sells beverages".

    **Deliverable:** Pearson/Spearman of unit price vs quantity (lines, excl. bulk buyers); correlation of regional weekly temperature with Beverages, Frozen and Hair Care units.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Price–quantity Pearson -0.076, Spearman -0.082. Temperature r: Beverages 0.463, Frozen 0.447, Hair Care 0.17. *(tolerance: ± 0.02)*

        **Model answer:** Across all products, price and quantity are confounded by product type (expensive items are bought one at a time), so a near-zero correlation says nothing about elasticity (Day 8 measures it properly). Temperature correlates with Hair Care even though the data contain no temperature effect on it — shared seasonality and region size.

        **Common wrong answer:** 'Price doesn't matter' and 'heat sells shampoo'.

    **Leader drill:** What design would you ask for before believing a weather effect?

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F1-13 · Basic · Income and limit"
    **Business framing:** How closely does the bank tie limits to income?

    **Deliverable:** Pearson and Spearman correlation of income with limit/loan amount.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Pearson 0.96, Spearman 0.898. *(tolerance: ± 0.01)*

        **Model answer:** Very strong — limits are set from income. Expect multicollinearity if both enter a model (F06, Day 5).

        **Common wrong answer:** Concluding income causes default via limit without a model.

    **Leader drill:** Which of the two would you keep in a scorecard, and why?

??? example "F1-14 · Applied · Weak correlation, strong risk"
    **Business framing:** Utilisation hardly correlates with default, so we can drop it.

    **Deliverable:** Point-biserial correlation of utilisation with default; default ratio for >90% vs ≤50% utilisation.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** r = 0.1; default ratio 6.129×. *(tolerance: ± 0.02)*

        **Model answer:** Correlation measures linear association with a rare binary outcome — it is small even when risk is six times higher in one band. Look at binned rates.

        **Common wrong answer:** Dropping a feature because |r| is small.

    **Leader drill:** Write the coaching note for the junior who proposed dropping it.

## 11. Recap

Plot first; choose the coefficient for the shape; check binned rates for binary outcomes; use control categories to expose
