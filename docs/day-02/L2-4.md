**Objectives** — you can: distinguish population, sample, parameter and statistic; choose simple random, stratified, cluster or
systematic sampling; recognise selection, survivorship, convenience and non-response bias; detect Simpson's paradox; and name the
confounders that could explain a comparison.

## 1. Business question

=== "Retail"

    > "The dashboard says promotions reduce units per line, Snacks grew strongly, and loyalty members spend far more. **Which of these is true?**"

=== "Finance"

    > "Can we estimate portfolio risk from an easy-to-pull sample — and learn about risk below our approval cut-off?"

## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 Vocabulary

    **Population** (all accounts) → **sample** (the ones you analyse) → **statistic** (sample default rate) estimating a
    **parameter** (true default rate, 4.0%). Sampling error is random; **bias** is systematic and does not shrink
    with more data.

    ### 2.2 Sampling designs

    | Design | How | When |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Simple random | every unit equally likely | default |
    | Stratified | sample within groups, combine with weights | guarantee subgroup sizes; gain precision when groups differ a lot |
    | Cluster | sample whole groups (stores, branches) | cheaper fieldwork; less precise |
    | Systematic | every k-th unit | easy; dangerous if the list has a cycle |

    In 2,000 simulated samples of 500 accounts, the SE of the default rate was 0.867 pp (simple random) vs
    0.868 pp (stratified by channel) — almost no gain, because default varies mostly *within* channels. Stratify to
    control subgroup sizes, not as a guaranteed precision boost.

    ### 2.3 Biases

    | Bias | Course example | Effect |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Convenience** | branch accounts only: 2.86% default vs 4.0% | risk understated |
    | **Selection** | model on booked accounts only (declined scored 498.0 vs booked 566.0) | no evidence below the cut-off |
    | **Survivorship** | Snacks growth on current SKUs 15.9% vs true -17.0% | growth invented |
    | **Self-selection** | loyalty members' baskets 37.7% bigger — but big spenders join | effect overstated |
    | **Non-response** | household size missing for large households (L1.3) | profiles skewed |

    ### 2.4 Simpson's paradox

    Units per line: promo lines are **-15.8%** vs non-promo overall — yet within every format promo lines sell
    more (Express 19% · Hyper 22.1% · Super 24.2%). Why: promotions are concentrated in Express stores (Express 62.8% · Hyper 4.9% · Super 11.9%
    of lines on promo), where baskets are small. The format mix, not the promotion, drives the pooled result.

    ### 2.5 Confounding

    A **confounder** affects both the "treatment" and the outcome. Displays run on 92.6% of promo lines vs
    3.0% of others — any "promotion effect" includes the display effect. Spend propensity drives both loyalty
    membership and basket size. Name confounders before trusting any observational comparison.

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        Bias does not shrink with n. Ask how the sample was drawn and who is missing. Simpson's paradox: a mix variable reverses the pooled
        comparison. Confounders explain associations without causation.

    **Judgement points**

    - **"Where did the sample come from?"** is the first question for any estimate. Convenience samples (one branch, one region, recent
      customers) systematically mislead.
    - **Survivorship is built into most master data.** Current product lists, active customers and booked accounts all hide what failed.
    - **Below-cut-off risk is unknowable from booked data.** Learn it with a small, controlled approval test, not by extrapolation.
    - **Stratify every executive comparison** by the obvious mix variables (format, channel, vintage) before it goes on a slide.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        For any comparison, ask the junior: *"Name two things that differ between these groups besides the thing you're comparing."* Then make
        them show the comparison within those groups.

## 8. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R2-8 · Basic · Simpson's paradox"
    **Business framing:** A dashboard claims 'promotions reduce units per line'.

    **Deliverable:** Relative difference in quantity per line, promo vs non-promo, overall and within each format; promo share by format.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Overall -15.8%; within format — Express 19% · Hyper 22.1% · Super 24.2%; promo share — Express 62.8% · Hyper 4.9% · Super 11.9%. *(tolerance: ± 0.5 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Promotions are concentrated in Express stores, where baskets are small top-ups. Pooling formats reverses the effect; within every format, promo lines sell more units (R14).

        **Common wrong answer:** Reporting the pooled comparison.

    **Leader drill:** Which comparison do you allow on the dashboard, and how do you label it?

??? example "R2-9 · Applied · Survivorship bias in range reviews"
    **Business framing:** Snacks 'grew' year on year according to the current product master.

    **Deliverable:** Year-2 vs year-1 Snacks growth using current SKUs only vs including archived (delisted) SKUs.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Current range 15.9% vs true category -17.0%. *(tolerance: ± 0.5 pp)*

        **Model answer:** The delisted SKUs sold in year 1 and vanish from the current list, so the 'current range' comparison flatters growth (R16).

        **Common wrong answer:** Joining sales to the current master with an inner join.

    **Leader drill:** What data must every range review include?

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F2-8 · Applied · Simple random vs stratified sampling"
    **Business framing:** Estimate the default rate from samples of 500 accounts.

    **Deliverable:** Standard error over 2,000 simulated samples — simple random vs stratified by acquisition channel.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** SRS 0.867 pp vs stratified 0.868 pp (true rate 4.0%). *(tolerance: ± 0.05 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Stratification by channel barely helps here: most variation is within channels (a rare binary outcome). Its real value is guaranteeing enough partner-channel accounts to estimate that subgroup — use it to control subgroup sample sizes, not as a magic precision gain.

        **Common wrong answer:** Assuming stratification always shrinks the SE substantially.

    **Leader drill:** When is stratified sampling worth the complexity?

??? example "F2-9 · Basic · Selection bias — booked accounts only"
    **Business framing:** Credit wants to lower the approval cut-off using a model trained on booked accounts.

    **Deliverable:** Mean application score of booked vs declined; default rate of the lowest-scoring 10% of booked accounts.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Booked 566.0 vs declined 498.0 (5,240 declined); lowest-decile booked default 18.2%. *(tolerance: ± 1)*

        **Model answer:** Nobody below the old cut-off was approved, so performance there is unobserved. Extrapolating below the cut-off is guesswork (reject inference, F18).

        **Common wrong answer:** Assuming declined applicants would behave like the lowest booked decile.

    **Leader drill:** How would you learn about risk below the cut-off safely?

??? example "F2-15 · Basic · A convenience sample"
    **Business framing:** A junior estimates the default rate using branch-acquired accounts only ("they were easiest to pull").

    **Deliverable:** Default rate in branch accounts vs all accounts.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Branch 2.86% vs all 4.0%. *(tolerance: ± 0.05 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Convenience samples inherit the selection of whoever is easy to reach. The branch book excludes the riskier partner channel and understates portfolio risk by more than a quarter.

        **Common wrong answer:** Assuming any large sample is representative.

    **Leader drill:** What sampling frame would you insist on?
