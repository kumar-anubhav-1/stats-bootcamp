## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 Rules you will use every day

    | Rule | Formula | Example |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Complement | P(not A) = 1 − P(A) | P(no fraud) = 1 − 0.7% |
    | Addition | P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) | promo or display |
    | Conditional | P(A \| B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B) | return rate within Electronics |
    | Multiplication | P(A and B) = P(A \| B) × P(B) | — |
    | Independence | P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) | testable from data |
    | Law of total probability | P(A) = Σ P(A \| Bᵢ) P(Bᵢ) | overall default = weighted channel defaults |
    | Expected value | E[X] = Σ x · P(x) | expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD |

    **Conditioning changes everything.** The return rate is 3.79% overall, **15.2%** for
    Electronics and 3.61% for fresh food.

    **Independence is an empirical question.** Promotions run on 20.1% of sales lines and displays on
    21.1%. If independent, both would occur on 4.24% of lines; they actually co-occur on
    **18.66%** — P(display | promo) = 92.6%.

    ### 2.2 Bayes' rule

    \[ P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{alert}) = \frac{P(\text{alert} \mid \text{fraud})\,P(\text{fraud})}{P(\text{alert} \mid \text{fraud})\,P(\text{fraud}) + P(\text{alert} \mid \text{no fraud})\,P(\text{no fraud})} \]

    In plain English: *of all alerts, what share are real?* It depends heavily on how rare fraud is.

    For the alert `inquiries_6m ≥ 4`: sensitivity 37.7%, false-positive rate 3.1%,
    base rate 0.7% → **P(fraud | alert) = 7.83%**. Counting directly in the data
    gives the same answer (7.83%).

    ### 2.3 Natural frequencies — the way to explain it

    Of **10,000** accounts, about **70** are fraudulent; the alert catches **26** of them — and
    also fires on **311** genuine customers. So most alerts are false, even though the alert raises the fraud rate
    11.2-fold.

    ### 2.4 Confusing the inverse

    P(partner | default) = 36.6%, but P(default | partner) = **13.49%**. Swapping them
    is one of the most common errors in business reporting.

    ### 2.5 Odds and log-odds

    Odds = p ÷ (1 − p). Partner default odds 0.1559 vs branch 0.0294; log-odds of partner default
    = -1.859. Logistic regression (Day 5) works on the log-odds scale; odds ratios compare groups.

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        Condition before you compare. Independence is testable. Bayes: the share of alerts that are real depends on the base rate as
        much as on the alert's accuracy. Always translate into natural frequencies. P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A).

    **Judgement points**

    - **Every alert, screen or test is a Bayes problem.** Ask for sensitivity, false-positive rate *and* base rate — then the cases per
      10,000. An alert with 37.7% sensitivity produces about 12
      false alarms per fraud caught here.
    - **Capacity is the real constraint.** 3.37% of accounts alerting means 337
      investigations per 10,000 accounts. Is that affordable, and what is a caught fraud worth?
    - **Policy statements often swap conditionals.** "Most defaulters came through partners" does not mean "partner customers mostly default".
    - **Dependence between levers** (promo and display) means their effects cannot be separated from observational data.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        When a junior quotes an accuracy, sensitivity or "% of X who are Y", ask them to restate it as "out of 10,000 customers, how many …".
        Errors in conditioning become visible immediately.

## 3. When to use, pitfalls

| Situation | Do | Pitfall |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Screening / alerts | Bayes with base rate, natural frequencies | quoting sensitivity as precision |
| Comparing groups | conditional rates per group | overall rates hiding group differences |
| Combining levers | test independence | assuming independence |
| Decisions under risk | expected value + spread | expected value alone (see L2.5) |

## 5. Output anatomy — an alert evaluation

| Number | Meaning | Good / suspicious |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base rate 0.7% | how rare the event is | decisive for precision |
| Sensitivity 37.7% | share of frauds caught | higher is better, at a cost in alerts |
| False-positive rate 3.1% | share of genuine customers alerted | small % × huge population = many alerts |
| Precision (PPV) 7.83% | share of alerts that are fraud | usually low for rare events — normal, not a bug |
| Lift 11.2× | precision ÷ base rate | how much better than random |

## 9. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R2-1 · Basic · Conditional probability of a return"
    **Business framing:** The returns desk asks: is a line more likely to be returned if it is Electronics?

    **Deliverable:** Return rate (returns ÷ sales lines) overall, for Electronics, and for fresh food (Fresh Produce, Dairy, Bakery).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Overall 3.79%; Electronics 15.2%; fresh food 3.61%. *(tolerance: ± 0.1 pp)*

        **Model answer:** P(return | Electronics) is about four times P(return | fresh food). Conditioning on category changes the answer — the overall rate describes neither.

        **Common wrong answer:** Quoting the overall rate for every category.

    **Leader drill:** Which categories would you prioritise for a returns-policy review, and what else would you check first?

??? example "R2-2 · Applied · Are promotion and display independent?"
    **Business framing:** Merchandising plans to evaluate promotions and displays separately.

    **Deliverable:** P(promo), P(display), observed P(both) vs P(promo) × P(display); P(display | promo).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** P(promo) 20.1%, P(display) 21.1%; P(both) 18.66% vs 4.24% if independent; P(display | promo) 92.6%. *(tolerance: ± 0.2 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Far from independent: displays almost always accompany promotions. Their effects cannot be separated from this data without a design that varies them separately (R15, Day 4).

        **Common wrong answer:** Estimating the display effect as if displays were assigned independently.

    **Leader drill:** What test design would you ask merchandising to run?

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F2-1 · Basic · Bayes and the fraud alert"
    **Business framing:** A proposed alert fires when `inquiries_6m ≥ 4`.

    **Deliverable:** Base rate, sensitivity, false-positive rate; P(fraud | alert) by Bayes' rule and directly; natural frequencies per 10,000 accounts.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Base 0.7%, sensitivity 37.7%, FPR 3.1% → P(fraud | alert) = 7.83% (direct: 7.83%). Per 10,000: 70 frauds, 26 caught, 311 false alarms. *(tolerance: ± 0.1 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Even a rule that raises the fraud rate 11.2-fold produces mostly false alarms, because fraud is rare. Natural frequencies make this obvious to non-statisticians.

        **Common wrong answer:** Reading sensitivity (37.7%) as 'the chance an alert is fraud'.

    **Leader drill:** How many investigators does this alert need per 10,000 accounts, and is it worth it?

??? example "F2-2 · Applied · Confusing the inverse"
    **Business framing:** A report says '36.6% of defaulters came through the partner channel' and concludes partner customers are '36.6% likely to default'.

    **Deliverable:** P(partner | default), P(default | partner), P(partner); odds and log-odds of default for partner vs branch.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** P(partner | default) 36.6%; P(default | partner) 13.49%; P(partner) 10.9%. Odds 0.1559 vs 0.0294; log-odds partner -1.859. *(tolerance: ± 0.1)*

        **Model answer:** P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A). Partner customers default at 13.49% — high, but nowhere near 36.6%. Bayes links them through the base rates.

        **Common wrong answer:** Treating the two conditional probabilities as interchangeable.

    **Leader drill:** Rewrite the report's sentence correctly.

??? example "F2-14 · Applied · Expected value — expected loss per account"
    **Business framing:** Illustrative: EAD = limit for cards, 60% of principal for loans; LGD 60%; PD = portfolio default rate.

    **Deliverable:** Average EAD and expected loss per account.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** EAD 15,057; EL ≈ 361 per account. *(tolerance: ± 5)*

        **Model answer:** Expected value = probability × amount. EL = PD × LGD × EAD is the same idea (Day 8). It averages outcomes; it says nothing about how bad a bad year can be (F2-10).

        **Common wrong answer:** Multiplying PD by limit without LGD.

    **Leader drill:** Which of the three inputs is most uncertain for this book?
