## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 The continuous family

    | Distribution | Shape / support | Typical use | Course example |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Normal** | symmetric, all reals | measurement error, averages | bureau score: 67.6% within 1 SD, 95.8% within 2 SD |
    | **Standard normal** | mean 0, SD 1 | z-scores, critical values (±1.96) | — |
    | **Lognormal** | positive, right-skewed; log is normal | spend, income | basket value (best AIC) |
    | **Exponential** | positive; constant hazard | time between events | baseline for gaps |
    | **Gamma** | positive, flexible skew | claim severity | best for attritional claims |
    | **Weibull** | positive; hazard rises (k > 1) or falls (k < 1) | time to churn/failure | visit gaps: k = 1.12 |
    | **Beta** | between 0 and 1 | uncertainty about a rate | Bayesian A/B (Day 4) |
    | **Uniform** | flat on an interval | simulation inputs | Monte Carlo (L2.5) |
    | **Laplace** | sharp peak, fat tails | price changes | Laplace AIC -10,855 vs normal -4,351 |
    | **Cauchy** | extremely heavy tails; **no mean** | a warning, not a model | running mean never settles |
    | **Student's t** | normal-like, heavier tails | small-sample means | critical value 2.571 (df 5), 2.042 (df 30) vs 1.96 |
    | **Chi-square** | sum of squared normals | variance, contingency tests | Day 3 |
    | **F** | ratio of chi-squares | ANOVA, regression | Day 3, 5 |

    <div class="sb-chart" data-chart="dist_lognormal" data-caption="Drag σ: the median stays at 2,400 while the mean and the tail grow — why insurers worry about σ."></div>

    ### 2.2 The family map (how they connect)

    ```mermaid
    flowchart LR
      B[Bernoulli] -->|sum of n| Bin[Binomial]
      Bin -->|n large, p small| P[Poisson]
      P -->|gamma-mixed rate| NB[Negative binomial]
      Bin -->|n large| N[Normal]
      P -->|times between events| E[Exponential]
      E -->|sum of k| G[Gamma]
      E -->|changing hazard| W[Weibull]
      N -->|exp of| LN[Lognormal]
      N -->|squares summed| C2[Chi-square]
      C2 -->|ratio| F[F]
      N -->|÷ sqrt chi-square| T[Student t]
      T -->|df = 1| CA[Cauchy]
      BE[Beta] -->|mixes p| BB[Beta-binomial]
    ```

    ### 2.3 Fitting and comparing

    1. Plot (log-scale histogram, ECDF).
    2. Fit candidates by maximum likelihood; compare **AIC** (lower is better, penalises parameters; ΔAIC > 10 is decisive).
    3. Check the **Q-Q plot** against the winner.
    4. Compare **tail quantiles** (P90, P99) with the data — the part that matters for risk.

    Basket value AIC: lognormal 30,648 · gamma 30,861 · weibull 31,051 · normal 35,632. Lognormal wins; the normal is far behind.

    ### 2.4 When one distribution is not enough

    Claim severity (catastrophic claims above 100,000 excluded): AIC gamma 12,591 · lognormal 12,598 · exponential 12,601 — practically tied, gamma shape
    1.19. The gamma's P99 is 14,517, but the empirical P99 of all claims is
    **19,085**, and three catastrophic claims raise mean severity from 3,440 to
    4,856. Model **attritional and large losses separately**.

    ### 2.5 Heavy tails can break averages

    For a Cauchy variable the running mean after 1,000 draws was -0.01 and after 100,000 was
    0.96 — it never settles, because there is no mean. Real data are rarely Cauchy, but very heavy tails make
    sample means unstable in the same way.

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        Amounts → lognormal/gamma; durations → exponential/Weibull; rates → beta; symmetric errors → normal; fat-tailed changes → Laplace/t.
        Compare fits on AIC *and* tails. A single distribution rarely fits catastrophic losses.

    **Judgement points**

    - **The tail decides the money.** Reserves, VaR and safety stock depend on high quantiles. Ask for fitted vs empirical P99 — not only AIC.
    - **"Normal" is an assumption to check.** Bureau scores are close; spend, income, balances and claims are not.
    - **Large-loss policy is a business decision**: cap, separate model, reinsurance. Analysts should surface it, not hide it.
    - **Small samples need t, not z**: critical value 2.571 with 5 degrees of freedom vs 1.96.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        Ask: *"Show me the fitted and empirical P99 side by side."* If the junior has not looked at the tail, the fit is not reviewed.

## 5. Output anatomy — `rcp_distribution_fit`

| Column | Meaning | Read as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AIC / ΔAIC | fit vs complexity | ΔAIC < 2 tie, > 10 decisive |
| KS distance | max gap between fitted and empirical CDF | smaller is better; p-values meaningless at large n |
| fitted P99 vs empirical P99 | tail accuracy | big gap → separate tail model |
| row accounting "exclude values above" | treated separately | must be stated in any report |

## 8. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R2-5 · Basic · Fit a distribution to basket value"
    **Business framing:** The pricing team wants a parametric model of basket value.

    **Deliverable:** Run `retail_fit_basket_value.yaml`: AIC for lognormal, gamma, Weibull, normal.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** AIC — lognormal 30,648 · gamma 30,861 · weibull 31,051 · normal 35,632. Best: lognormal. *(tolerance: ± 50 AIC)*

        **Model answer:** Positive, right-skewed spend is typically lognormal; the normal is far worse. Check the Q-Q plot: the best family can still miss the extreme tail (bulk buyers).

        **Common wrong answer:** Picking normal because 'the CLT says so' — the CLT is about means, not individual values.

    **Leader drill:** When does the choice of distribution change a business decision?

??? example "R2-6 · Applied · Time between visits"
    **Business framing:** How long do customers wait between baskets?

    **Deliverable:** Median and mean gap (days) between a customer's consecutive baskets; Weibull shape k.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Median 137.0 days, mean 170.0; Weibull k = 1.12 (n = 1,867 gaps). *(tolerance: ± 0.05 for k)*

        **Model answer:** k ≈ 1 means a nearly constant chance of returning each day (close to exponential). k > 1: the chance rises with time since last visit; k < 1: the longer away, the less likely to return. Customers who never return are missing from gaps — censoring (Day 9).

        **Common wrong answer:** Ignoring that only customers with two or more baskets contribute gaps.

    **Leader drill:** Why would gap analysis understate churn?

??? example "R2-7 · Stretch · Peaked, fat-tailed price changes"
    **Business framing:** Weekly log price changes in the price extract (non-zero changes only).

    **Deliverable:** AIC of Laplace vs normal fits; excess kurtosis.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** AIC Laplace -10,855 vs normal -4,351; kurtosis 2.4. *(tolerance: ± 50 AIC)*

        **Model answer:** Price changes are mostly small with occasional large jumps (promotions) — a sharp peak and fat tails that the Laplace captures better than the normal.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using a normal model and underestimating big price moves.

    **Leader drill:** Where in pricing risk would fat tails matter?

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F2-6 · Basic · Claim severity"
    **Business framing:** Run `finance_fit_claim_severity.yaml` (catastrophic claims above 100,000 excluded).

    **Deliverable:** AIC by family; gamma shape; fitted vs empirical P99 (all claims); mean with and without catastrophic claims.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** AIC — gamma 12,591 · lognormal 12,598 · exponential 12,601 (best gamma, shape 1.19). P99: gamma 14,517 vs empirical 19,085. Mean 4,856 (all) vs 3,440. *(tolerance: ± 10 AIC)*

        **Model answer:** The candidates are close on AIC, and the best fit under-states the empirical tail, which is driven by three catastrophic claims. Model attritional and large losses separately (large-loss loading).

        **Common wrong answer:** Fitting one distribution to all claims including catastrophes.

    **Leader drill:** What would you ask the pricing actuary about large losses?

??? example "F2-7 · Applied · Is the bureau score normal?"
    **Business framing:** Check the 68–95 rule.

    **Deliverable:** Share of accounts within 1 and 2 SD of the mean bureau score.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** 67.6% within 1 SD; 95.8% within 2 SD. *(tolerance: ± 0.3 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Close to the normal's 68% / 95% — the score is roughly symmetric (skew 0.23). Approximate normality is enough for many purposes; check tails before relying on extreme quantiles.

        **Common wrong answer:** Running a normality test on 25,000 rows and declaring the score 'not normal' as if that mattered.

    **Leader drill:** When does approximate normality matter for credit decisions?
