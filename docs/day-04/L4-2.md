## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 CUPED in one sentence

    Subtract from each unit's outcome the part predictable from its **pre-experiment** behaviour:

    $$Y^{\text{cuped}} = Y - \theta\,(X - \bar X), \qquad \theta = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(Y, X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}$$

    The treatment effect is unchanged on average (randomisation makes X balanced) but the noise from "big stores are big" disappears.
    Variance falls by **ρ²**, where ρ is the correlation between X and Y. ANCOVA (regress Y on treatment + X) does the same job.

    ### 2.2 Store test: a strong covariate

    Post-period weekly Hair Care units vs the same store's pre-period units: **ρ = 0.98**.

    | Analysis (unit = store) | Difference (units/week) | SE | p |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Raw post-period means | 7.17 | 9.18 | 0.44 |
    | **CUPED** (θ = 1.051) | **6.48** | **1.51** | **0.00012** |
    | ANCOVA (post ~ test + pre) | — | — | 0.00014 |

    Variance reduction **96.0%**; the SE is **6.1× smaller**. CUPED lift
    17.3% (95% CI 9.2 – 25.5%) — consistent with the
    growth-index analysis of L3.4 (13.6%, CI 5.5 – 21.7%) and with the planted +12%.
    The raw comparison, which ignores store size, finds nothing.

    ### 2.3 E-coupon: a weak covariate

    Correlation of log pre-period spend with 14-day conversion: **ρ = 0.118** → variance reduction only
    **1.4%** (SE 0.735 → 0.73 pp). Worth doing (it's free), but no miracle.
    Rule of thumb: CUPED saves ρ² of the sample — 1.4% here, 96.0% for stores.

    !!! warning "The covariate must be measured before assignment"
        Using anything the treatment could have affected (in-test visits, post-assignment spend) biases the estimate.

    ### 2.4 Peeking and sequential designs

    The e-coupon test (clean data) accrued users over 14 days. Cumulative z-statistics by day:

    | Day | Cumulative z | OBF boundary | Naive (|z| > 1.96) | OBF |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | 1 | 0.01 | 7.96 | · | · |
    | 2 | 1.12 | 5.63 | · | · |
    | 3 | 1.63 | 4.52 | · | · |
    | 4 | 1.71 | 3.92 | · | · |
    | 5 | 2.27 | 3.52 | stop | · |
    | 6 | 2.72 | 3.19 | stop | · |
    | 7 | 2.85 | 2.96 | stop | · |
    | 8 | 2.44 | 2.78 | stop | · |
    | 9 | 2.32 | 2.62 | stop | · |
    | 10 | 2.1 | 2.48 | stop | · |
    | 11 | 1.95 | 2.37 | · | · |
    | 12 | 2.08 | 2.27 | stop | · |
    | 13 | 2.25 | 2.18 | stop | stop |
    | 14 | 2.33 | 2.11 | stop | stop |
    
    - **Naive peeking** (stop at the first |z| > 1.96): stops on **day 5**; 9 of
      14 daily looks were "significant", and the z-statistic later dipped back below 1.96. In an A/A setting this rule
      gives ~18.1% false positives (L3.7).
    - **O'Brien-Fleming** boundaries (z_k = 2.11/√t_k, calibrated so the overall false-positive rate stays 5%) are very
      strict early (day 1: 7.96) and close to 1.96 at the end (day 14: 2.11).
      The test crosses on **day 13**.

    Alternatives: Pocock (constant boundary), alpha-spending functions (Lan-DeMets) for unplanned look times, and always-valid
    p-values (mSPRT) used by some experimentation platforms.

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        CUPED uses pre-period behaviour to remove predictable noise: big gains when the covariate is strongly correlated (stores),
        small when weak (conversion). Peeking without a plan inflates false positives; sequential designs let you monitor safely.

    **Judgement points**

    - **CUPED is the cheapest sample you'll ever buy.** For store tests it can turn an impossible test (L3.6) into a feasible one.
      Make it the platform default whenever a pre-period exists.
    - **Monitoring is fine; deciding is the problem.** Dashboards may update daily, but the stopping rule must be fixed in advance.
    - **Stopping early for success overstates the effect** (winner's curse) — expect shrinkage on rollout.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        Ask: *"What covariate did you use, measured when, and how much variance did it remove? What was the planned stopping rule?"*

## 5. Output anatomy — CUPED rows in `rcp_ab_test`

| Element | Read as |
| --- | --- |
| θ | how much of the covariate is subtracted (≈ 1 when outcome and covariate are on the same scale) |
| variance reduction | ≈ ρ²; the share of sample you saved |
| CUPED difference | should be close to the unadjusted difference on average; much narrower CI |
| looks.png / cumulative_looks.csv | descriptive; compare with a pre-planned boundary |

## 6. Scenario matrix

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    | Situation | Use |
    | --- | --- |
    | pre-period outcome available, ρ > 0.5 | CUPED / ANCOVA — large gain |
    | weak covariate (ρ < 0.2) | CUPED harmless, small gain |
    | covariate measured after assignment | do **not** adjust |
    | must monitor and possibly stop early | O'Brien-Fleming / alpha spending |

## 8. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R4-4 · Basic · CUPED on the store test"
    **Business framing:** The raw post-period comparison of the Hair Care test shows nothing.

    **Deliverable:** Run `retail_store_test_cuped.yaml`; report ρ(pre, post), θ, variance reduction, raw and CUPED effects.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** ρ = 0.98; θ = 1.051; variance reduction 96.0%; raw +7.17 (p = 0.44) vs CUPED +6.48 units/week (p = 0.00012). *(tolerance: ± 0.1)*

        **Model answer:** Store size dominates raw variation. Adjusting each store by its own pre-period removes ~96% of variance and reveals the lift.

        **Common wrong answer:** Concluding from the raw comparison that the feature failed.

    **Leader drill:** Should CUPED be the default for all store tests? Any risks?

??? example "R4-5 · Applied · CUPED on conversion — a weak covariate"
    **Business framing:** The digital team expects CUPED to halve the e-coupon test's sample size.

    **Deliverable:** Correlation of log pre-period spend with conversion; variance reduction; SE before and after (clean data).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** ρ = 0.118; variance reduction 1.4%; SE 0.735 → 0.73 pp. *(tolerance: ± 0.5 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Variance reduction ≈ ρ² ≈ 1.4%. Spend history barely predicts a 14-day conversion; look for a better covariate (previous coupon redemption, visit frequency).

        **Common wrong answer:** Promising large savings without checking ρ.

    **Leader drill:** Which covariates would you ask the data team to log for future app tests?

??? example "R4-6 · Stretch · When could the test have stopped?"
    **Business framing:** Marketing asks why the test ran 14 days when it was "significant on day 5".

    **Deliverable:** Daily cumulative z and naive p; O'Brien-Fleming boundaries for the observed looks; first stopping day for each rule.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Naive: day 5 (9 of 14 looks significant; z later dips below 1.96). OBF (c = 2.11): day 13. *(tolerance: ± 1 day)*

        **Model answer:** Day-5 significance came from looking every day. A planned OBF design would have stopped on day 13 with a valid 5% error rate.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using 1.96 at every look.

    **Leader drill:** Write the stopping rule for the next app test.

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F4-4 · Basic · Planning weekly looks"
    **Business framing:** Collections wants weekly results.

    **Deliverable:** For 8 equally spaced looks, compute O'Brien-Fleming boundaries (z_k = c/√t_k with c calibrated to 5%).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Boundaries fall from about 5.6–5.9 at look 1 to about 2.0–2.1 at look 8. *(tolerance: ± 0.1)*

        **Model answer:** Early looks need overwhelming evidence; the final look is close to 1.96, so little power is lost compared with a single analysis.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using 1.96 at every look (false positives ~15–20%).

    **Leader drill:** What would you tell the team about early 'wins'?

??? example "F4-5 · Applied · CUPED for cure rate"
    **Business framing:** Could pre-period balance and DPD history reduce the challenger test's variance?

    **Deliverable:** Explain which covariates are valid for CUPED in collections and estimate the possible variance reduction from their correlation with cure.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Valid: covariates measured before assignment (balance at entry, previous delinquency, bureau score). Variance reduction ≈ ρ² — typically small for a binary outcome unless a covariate is strongly predictive. *(tolerance: —)*

        **Model answer:** Use a regression adjustment (ANCOVA) with several pre-assignment covariates; it is never worse than unadjusted in large samples and can shorten the test.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using payments made after assignment as a covariate.

    **Leader drill:** Would you approve a test that adjusts for 20 covariates? What safeguards?

??? example "F4-6 · Stretch · Winner's curse"
    **Business framing:** A test stopped early for success shows +9 pp; after rollout the gain is +5 pp.

    **Deliverable:** Explain why early-stopped effects are biased upward and how to correct expectations.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Stopping when the estimate is high selects overestimates; expect shrinkage. Use conservative (bias-adjusted) estimates or a holdout to measure the post-launch effect. *(tolerance: —)*

        **Model answer:** Early stopping truncates the sampling distribution at the boundary — the reported effect is conditional on having crossed it.

        **Common wrong answer:** Blaming the rollout for 'losing' 4 pp.

    **Leader drill:** How would you budget for this effect in business cases?

## 10. Recap

CUPED: Y − θ(X − X̄), variance × (1 − ρ²), pre-period covariates only. Sequential: plan the looks; OBF is strict early, lenient late.
