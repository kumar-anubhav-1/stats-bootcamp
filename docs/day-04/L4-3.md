## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 Prior × likelihood → posterior

    - **Prior:** what we believed before the data (from history, other tests, expert judgement).
    - **Likelihood:** how probable the observed data are for each possible value.
    - **Posterior:** updated belief = prior × likelihood (normalised). With more data the likelihood dominates.

    For rates, the **Beta–Binomial** pair makes this arithmetic: prior Beta(α, β) + k successes in n trials → posterior
    Beta(α + k, β + n − k). Beta(α, β) behaves like "α successes and β failures already seen".

    ### 2.2 Bayesian A/B on the e-coupon (clean data)

    Uniform priors Beta(1, 1) for each arm, 200,000 posterior draws:

    | Quantity | Value | Meaning |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | P(treatment > control) | **99.0%** | probability the coupon is better, given data and prior |
    | Posterior mean lift | 1.71 pp | |
    | 95% credible interval | 0.27 to 3.16 pp | 95% probability the lift lies here (given the model) |
    | P(lift > 1 pp) | 83.2% | probability of clearing the practical threshold |
    | Expected loss if we ship | 0.002 pp | average conversion we give up if the coupon is actually worse |
    | Expected loss if we keep control | 1.714 pp | what we forgo by not shipping |

    **Decision rule:** ship when expected loss of shipping is below a pre-agreed "threshold of caring" (e.g. 0.1 pp).

    With flat priors the credible interval matches the confidence interval (0.27 to 3.15); the
    *interpretation* differs. A credible interval is a probability statement about the parameter; a confidence interval is a
    statement about the procedure (L3.1).

    ### 2.3 Priors matter when data are thin

    **Sceptical prior.** Past coupon tests rarely moved conversion by more than ~1 pp: prior on the lift centred at +0.2 pp, SD 0.4 pp.
    Posterior mean **0.55 pp** (95% interval -0.14 to 1.23),
    P(better) 94.0%. A sceptical prior shrinks surprising results — a defence against the winner's curse,
    at the price of being slow to believe genuinely big effects. **State and justify priors before seeing data; show sensitivity to them.**

    ### 2.4 A new segment with few accounts

    Students acquired through the online partner in 2025Q3: **27 defaults out of 137** = 19.71%
    (Wilson CI 13.91 – 27.16%). Prior: historical student default rate 3.82%,
    weighted as 100 accounts' worth of evidence.

    | | Default rate | Interval |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Prior (history) | 3.82% | — |
    | Data only | 19.71% | 13.91 – 27.16% |
    | **Posterior** | **13.01%** | 9.04 – 17.56% |

    P(default rate > 10%) = **92.4%**. Even after shrinkage towards history, this segment is far riskier than past
    students — the latest vintages are different (L3.3, L9.6). The prior weight is a judgement: 100 accounts' worth says "history is
    relevant, but not decisive". Show results for other weights.

    ### 2.5 Beyond conjugate pairs

    For models without neat formulas (hierarchical store effects, many parameters) probabilistic-programming tools such as PyMC or
    Stan draw from the posterior by simulation (MCMC). Ask Claude Code for a PyMC model only when the conjugate or normal approximation
    is not enough, and always check convergence diagnostics (R-hat ≈ 1, effective sample size).

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        Bayesian readouts answer the question executives ask — "how likely is it better, and what do we risk?" — at the cost of
        stating a prior. With lots of data, Bayesian and frequentist answers agree; with little data, the prior matters and must be
        defended.

    **Judgement points**

    - **Use P(better) and expected loss in decision memos.** "99.0% likely better; shipping risks at most
      0.002 pp on average" is clearer than "p = 0.0197".
    - **Bayesian does not fix bad data.** The SRM-broken test gives a misleading posterior too.
    - **Priors are governance.** Agree default priors per test type in advance; never choose them after seeing results.
    - **Small segments need shrinkage.** New products, branches and segments should be planned on posterior estimates, not on
      their first raw rate.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        Ask: *"What prior did you use, why, and how does the conclusion change with a flat prior and a sceptical one?"*

## 5. Output anatomy — Bayesian section

| Element | Read as |
| --- | --- |
| P(treatment better) | not a p-value; 50% = no idea, 99% = strong evidence |
| credible interval | 95% probability range for the lift under the model and prior |
| expected loss if ship / keep | average regret of each choice; ship when "ship" loss is tiny |
| prior statement | must be shown; flat priors ≈ frequentist answer |

## 6. Scenario matrix

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    | Situation | Approach |
    | --- | --- |
    | large A/B test, no strong prior | flat prior — same answer as frequentist, better language |
    | many similar past tests | informative / sceptical prior from their results |
    | tiny segment, relevant history | Beta prior with modest weight (shrinkage) |
    | complex hierarchical model | PyMC/Stan, with diagnostics |

## 8. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R4-7 · Basic · Bayesian readout"
    **Business framing:** The CMO wants 'the probability the coupon works'.

    **Deliverable:** Beta(1,1) posteriors per arm (clean data): P(treatment better), credible interval of the lift, expected loss of each decision.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** P(better) 99.0%; 95% interval 0.27 to 3.16 pp; loss if ship 0.002 pp, if keep 1.714 pp. *(tolerance: ± 1 pp on P(better))*

        **Model answer:** Very likely better; shipping risks almost nothing on average, keeping control forgoes ~1.7 pp. Ship — after the logging fix confirms it.

        **Common wrong answer:** Calling P(better) a p-value.

    **Leader drill:** Write the one-sentence version for the CMO.

??? example "R4-8 · Applied · A sceptical prior"
    **Business framing:** Past coupon tests rarely moved conversion by more than 1 pp.

    **Deliverable:** Normal prior on the lift (mean 0.2 pp, SD 0.4 pp) combined with the clean data: posterior mean, interval, P(better).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Posterior mean 0.55 pp (-0.14 to 1.23), P(better) 94.0%. *(tolerance: ± 0.1 pp)*

        **Model answer:** The sceptical prior pulls the estimate towards typical coupon effects. A strong prior is a deliberate guard against the winner's curse; show both.

        **Common wrong answer:** Picking the prior after seeing which gives the preferred answer.

    **Leader drill:** Who should own default priors for the experimentation platform?

??? example "R4-9 · Stretch · Expected loss as a stopping rule"
    **Business framing:** Product wants a Bayesian stopping rule.

    **Deliverable:** Explain an expected-loss rule ("stop and ship when expected loss of shipping < 0.1 pp") and its risks.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** At the end of the clean test, expected loss of shipping = 0.002 pp < 0.1 pp → ship. *(tolerance: —)*

        **Model answer:** Expected-loss rules control regret, not false positives; with frequent checks they still favour early stops on noise. Pair with a minimum duration (two weekly cycles) and a minimum sample.

        **Common wrong answer:** Believing Bayesian tests are immune to peeking problems.

    **Leader drill:** What minimum conditions would you add?

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F4-7 · Basic · Bayesian challenger readout"
    **Business framing:** The Head of Collections asks 'how sure are we?'.

    **Deliverable:** P(challenger better), credible interval, expected loss of switching (flat priors).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** P(better) ≈ 99.9%; interval close to the CI 2.4–9.7 pp; expected loss of switching ≈ 0. *(tolerance: ± 0.2 pp)*

        **Model answer:** With flat priors and this sample the Bayesian and frequentist views agree; the Bayesian language is easier to act on.

        **Common wrong answer:** Saying 'p = 0.001 means 99.9% probability'.

    **Leader drill:** Which phrasing would you use with the board?

??? example "F4-8 · Applied · Prior for a new segment"
    **Business framing:** Pricing needs a planning default rate for students acquired via the online partner (2025Q3).

    **Deliverable:** Raw rate with Wilson CI; Beta prior from historical students (weight 100); posterior mean, 95% interval, P(rate > 10%).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Raw 19.71% (27/137, CI 13.91–27.16%); prior 3.82%; posterior 13.01% (9.04–17.56%); P(>10%) 92.4%. *(tolerance: ± 0.2 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Shrinkage moderates the raw rate, but the segment is clearly much riskier than history. Price on the posterior and review monthly.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using the historical rate because the sample is 'too small'.

    **Leader drill:** How would you choose the prior weight, and who signs it off?

??? example "F4-9 · Stretch · Prior sensitivity"
    **Business framing:** Model risk asks how much the prior drives the answer.

    **Deliverable:** Posterior mean and interval for prior weights 25, 100 and 400 accounts.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Heavier priors pull the estimate toward 3.82%; with weight 400 the posterior moves most of the way back to history. *(tolerance: —)*

        **Model answer:** Show the range. If decisions differ across reasonable priors, collect more data before committing.

        **Common wrong answer:** Reporting one prior only.

    **Leader drill:** What would you do if pricing decisions changed across the three priors?
