## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 The GLM recipe

    | Outcome | Family | Link | Coefficients read as |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | counts | Poisson / negative binomial | log | rate ratios (% change) |
    | positive, right-skewed amounts | Gamma | log | % change in the mean |
    | zeros + positive amounts (spend incl. non-buyers, pure premium) | Tweedie | log | % change |
    | yes/no | binomial | logit | odds ratios (L5.3) |

    **Exposure offset:** a policy observed for half a year has half the chance of a claim. Add log(exposure) as an offset so the model
    describes the claim *rate* per policy-year.

    ### 2.2 Over-dispersion in counts

    Beverages units per store-week (store effects, temperature, holiday, promo):

    | Model | Pearson dispersion | AIC | Temperature effect | SE |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Poisson | **5.38** | 49,559 | +1.87% | 0.0002 |
    | Negative binomial (α = 0.0312) | — | **38,307** | +1.92% | 0.00048 |

    Variance is 5.38× what Poisson assumes: the Poisson SEs are too small by roughly √5.38. Negative
    binomial fixes it (much lower AIC, honest SEs); the effects barely move. Rule: **check dispersion; > 1.5 → NB or robust SEs.**

    ### 2.3 Claim frequency (motor, 5,152 policies, 565 claims)

    Overall 0.1756 claims per policy-year. Negative binomial GLM with log(exposure) offset — rate ratios vs drivers aged
    18-24:

    | Driver age | Rate ratio |
    | --- | --- |
    | 25-34 | 0.6 |
    | 35-49 | 0.58 |
    | 50-64 | 0.58 |
    | 65+ | 0.84 |
    

    Each vehicle group step: ×1.075. Poisson dispersion here is only 1.13, but NB still fits better
    (AIC 3,616 vs 3,649): policyholders differ in risk beyond the rating factors (F17).

    ### 2.4 Severity: Gamma, and the large-loss problem

    Mean claim 4,856 across 692 claims; by type: collision 4,215 · fire 6,633 · glass 5,623 · theft 4,732 · water 6,694. A Gamma GLM (log link)
    models the mean by claim type. But the catastrophic claims (F17) dominate: capping at the 99th percentile (19,085)
    gives a mean of 3,462 — the uncapped mean is **40.3% higher**. Standard practice:
    model attritional (capped) severity with Gamma and add a **large-loss loading** priced separately.

    ### 2.5 Pure premium and loss ratio

    Pure premium = frequency × severity. Motor: **767.1 per policy-year** of claims. Loss ratios (claims ÷
    premium): home 0.82 · motor 1.227. **Motor pays out more than it collects.** Re-rating should start with the large-loss load and the youngest drivers, whose
    claim rate is about 1.7× that of drivers aged 25–34.

    ### 2.6 Two more tools

    - **Tweedie**: one model for "zero or positive" outcomes (spend including non-buyers, pure premium per policy) — power parameter
      1 < p < 2 between Poisson and Gamma.
    - **Quantile regression**: models a percentile (median, P90) instead of the mean — robust to outliers and useful when the tail is
      the decision (P90 basket for staffing, P95 claim for reserving).

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        GLMs match the model to the outcome: counts (Poisson/NB with exposure), positive amounts (Gamma), zero-inflated amounts
        (Tweedie), percentiles (quantile regression). Check over-dispersion; treat large losses separately.

    **Judgement points**

    - **A loss ratio above 1 is a pricing decision, not a modelling detail.** Motor needs re-rating (young drivers, vehicle groups)
      and an explicit large-loss load.
    - **Poisson SEs on over-dispersed data overstate certainty** — e.g. a promotion "significant" only because SEs are 2× too small.
    - **Ask which quantile matters.** Staffing, reserving and stock decisions often depend on P90/P95, not the mean.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        Ask: *"Which family and link, why, what's the dispersion, and how are large losses handled?"*

## 5. Output anatomy — GLM output in `rcp_regression`

| Element | Read as |
| --- | --- |
| % change per unit | exp(β) − 1 for log links |
| Pearson dispersion | ≈ 1 fine; > 1.5 over-dispersed (Poisson) |
| deviance, AIC | compare families on the same data |
| exposure | offset — rates per unit of exposure |

## 8. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R5-9 · Basic · Poisson or negative binomial for units?"
    **Business framing:** Model weekly Beverages units as counts.

    **Deliverable:** Poisson dispersion, AIC of Poisson vs NB, temperature effect and SE in each.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Dispersion 5.38; AIC 49,559 vs 38,307; temperature +1.87% (SE 0.0002) vs +1.92% (SE 0.00048). *(tolerance: ± 0.1)*

        **Model answer:** Over-dispersed: Poisson SEs are too small. NB fits much better with similar effects and honest SEs.

        **Common wrong answer:** Reporting Poisson p-values.

    **Leader drill:** Where else in your reporting might Poisson SEs be overconfident?

??? example "R5-10 · Applied · Quantile thinking for staffing"
    **Business framing:** Store operations staff for busy hours, not average hours.

    **Deliverable:** Explain how quantile regression of basket value (P90) on format and day of week would differ from OLS, and when you'd use it.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Quantile regression models a percentile directly; effects on P90 can differ from effects on the mean (bulk buyers). *(tolerance: —)*

        **Model answer:** Use when the decision depends on the tail (staffing, stock buffers); robust to extreme baskets.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using the mean basket to staff peak hours.

    **Leader drill:** Which of your KPIs should be a percentile?

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F5-9 · Basic · Claim frequency GLM"
    **Business framing:** Run `finance_claims_frequency.yaml`.

    **Deliverable:** Rate ratios by driver age and vehicle group; overall frequency; Poisson dispersion and AIC vs NB.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Frequency 0.1756/policy-year; rate ratios 25-34 0.6 · 35-49 0.58 · 50-64 0.58 · 65+ 0.84 (vs 18-24); vehicle group ×1.075; dispersion 1.13; AIC 3,649 vs 3,616. *(tolerance: ± 0.05)*

        **Model answer:** Young drivers claim ~1.7× as often; NB fits better than Poisson (unobserved heterogeneity).

        **Common wrong answer:** Fitting counts without the exposure offset.

    **Leader drill:** Which rating factor would you revisit first?

??? example "F5-10 · Applied · Large-loss loading"
    **Business framing:** Pricing uses mean severity.

    **Deliverable:** Mean severity, P99 cap, capped mean, loading.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Mean 4,856; cap 19,085; capped mean 3,462; loading 40.3%. *(tolerance: ± 1%)*

        **Model answer:** Model attritional severity with Gamma on capped claims and add a separate large-loss load.

        **Common wrong answer:** Dropping large claims from pricing.

    **Leader drill:** How often should the load be revisited?

??? example "F5-11 · Stretch · Is motor profitable?"
    **Business framing:** The board asks about product profitability.

    **Deliverable:** Loss ratio by product; motor pure premium per policy-year.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Loss ratios home 0.82 · motor 1.227; motor pure premium 767.1. *(tolerance: ± 0.01)*

        **Model answer:** Motor claims exceed premiums; re-rate (young drivers, vehicle group, large-loss load).

        **Common wrong answer:** Averaging products into one loss ratio.

    **Leader drill:** What is your target loss ratio, and how fast would you re-price?
