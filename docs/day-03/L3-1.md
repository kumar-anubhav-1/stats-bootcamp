**Objectives** — you can: distinguish a point estimate from an interval estimate; compute and read a confidence interval (CI) for a
mean, a proportion and a difference; explain what "95%" does and does not mean; choose Wilson over Wald for rare rates; see why heavy
tails break textbook intervals; and insist that every number in a report carries its uncertainty.

## 1. Business question

=== "Retail"

    > "What is our average basket value — and how sure are we?"

=== "Finance"

## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 Estimates, estimators and their errors

    A **parameter** is the true value in the population (the average basket of every shopper who could visit); a **statistic** is what
    we compute from the data we have. A good estimator is **unbiased** (right on average), **consistent** (closer as n grows) and
    **efficient** (small spread). The sample mean is all three for most business data — but it is not *robust*: one bulk buyer moves it.

    **Maximum likelihood (intuition).** Pick the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable. The sample proportion
    is the maximum-likelihood estimate of a rate; the lognormal fits of L2.3 were maximum-likelihood fits.

    ### 2.2 The confidence interval

    $$\text{estimate} \pm \text{critical value} \times \text{standard error}$$

    Mean basket value from 3,163 baskets: **50.52** (SD 67.58, SE
    1.2). 95% CI: **48.16 – 52.87**.

    | Level | Interval | Width |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | 90% | 48.54 – 52.49 | narrower: less confidence |
    | 95% | 48.16 – 52.87 | the default |
    | 99% | 47.42 – 53.61 | wider: more confidence |

    **What 95% means.** If we repeated the sampling many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would
    contain the true mean. It is a property of the *method*. It does **not** mean "95% of baskets lie in this range" (individual
    baskets range from under 5 to over 1,000) and, strictly, it does not mean "95% probability the true value is in *this* interval"
    (that is a Bayesian credible interval — L4.3).

    <div class="sb-chart" data-chart="ci_coverage" data-caption="Each line is one sample's 95% interval; red ones miss the true mean. Drag n: intervals shrink, but about 1 in 20 still miss."></div>

    ### 2.3 Width and sample size

    The SE shrinks with √n, so **four times the data halves the width**. Expected 95% CI width for mean basket value:

    | n | Width |
    | --- | --- |
    | 100 | 26.82 |
    | 400 | 13.29 |
    | 1,600 | 6.63 |

    ### 2.4 When the textbook interval lies: heavy tails

    We drew thousands of samples from our baskets and checked how often the t-interval caught the true mean:

    | n per sample | Actual coverage of a "95%" t-interval |
    | --- | --- |
    | 30 | **84.2%** |
    | 200 | **88.2%** |

    Bulk buyers (R07) make the distribution so skewed that most samples miss the big baskets — their interval is too narrow and sits
    too low — while the few samples that catch one get a very wide interval. Remedies: a **bootstrap** interval (L3.2), a log-scale analysis, or report the **median** with its CI.

    ### 2.5 Intervals for proportions — Wald vs Wilson

    The textbook **Wald** interval p̂ ± 1.96·√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n) breaks for rare events. At the fraud base rate
    (0.7%) with n = 300, a simulated "95%" Wald interval covered the truth only **88.1%** of the time —
    in 11.8% of samples there were zero frauds and Wald returned "0 to 0". The **Wilson** interval covered
    **94.5%**. Use Wilson (or exact Clopper-Pearson) for rates.

    | Segment | Defaults / n | Rate | 95% Wilson CI |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | All accounts | 1,000 / 25,000 | 4.0% | 3.76 – 4.25% |
    | Students | 89 / 1,374 | 6.48% | 5.29 – 7.9% |
    | Branch BR18 | 8 / 562 | 1.42% | 0.72 – 2.78% |

    The student interval lies entirely above the overall rate; the small branch's interval is almost four times wider than its rate
    would suggest — a branch league table without intervals would rank it "best" on eight defaults.

    ### 2.6 Interval for a difference

    The two-group CIs of L3.4 follow the same recipe: difference ± critical value × SE of the difference. Always report the
    difference **with** its interval (e.g. unverified income defaults 3.12 pp more, 95% CI 2.4
    – 3.83 pp).

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh"
        A CI is the range of values compatible with the data at a chosen confidence level. Width ∝ 1/√n. Heavy tails and rare events
        break textbook intervals; bootstrap or Wilson fix them. "95%" describes the method, not individual values.

    **Judgement points**

    - **Every KPI needs its interval.** A branch default rate of 1.42% on 562 accounts
      is compatible with anything from 0.72% to 2.78% — not evidence of better
      underwriting.
    - **Ask what the interval was computed on.** Lines inside baskets, months inside accounts and weeks inside stores are not
      independent; intervals computed on them are too narrow (L3.2).
    - **Precision is purchasable.** Halving an interval costs four times the data — decide what precision the decision needs first.

    !!! tip "Coaching card"
        When a junior reports "the average basket is 50.52", ask: *"Give me the interval, the n, and whether bulk buyers are in it."*

## 5. Output anatomy — an interval in a report

| Element | Example | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estimate | 50.52 | the best single guess |
| Interval and level | 48.16 – 52.87 (95%) | the range compatible with the data |
| n and unit | 3,163 baskets | determines width; must be the independent unit |
| Method | t-interval / Wilson / bootstrap | wrong method → wrong coverage |
| Caveat | heavy tail; bulk buyers included | tells the reader when to distrust it |

## 6. Scenario matrix

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    | Situation | Interval |
    | --- | --- |
    | mean of a roughly symmetric variable | t-interval |
    | mean of a heavy-tailed variable | bootstrap (BCa) or log scale; report median too |
    | proportion, n·p ≥ 10 | Wilson (Wald acceptable) |
    | rare proportion or small n | Wilson or Clopper-Pearson |
    | difference of two means / rates | Welch CI / Newcombe or Wald difference |
    | median, ratio, percentile | bootstrap |

=== "Leader"

    | Report says | You ask | Norm |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | a ranking of branches by rate | intervals and n for each | small units always land at the extremes |
    | "average up 3%" | CI of the change | a CI crossing 0 is not a change |
    | "95% of customers spend 48–53" | that's the mean's CI, not customers | CIs describe estimates |

## 8. Exercises

### Retail · NorthStar

??? example "R3-1 · Basic · Mean basket value with its interval"
    **Business framing:** The CFO wants "the average basket" for the annual report.

    **Deliverable:** Mean basket value with 90%, 95% and 99% t-intervals (one row per basket; returns, entry errors and duplicates removed); n.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** n = 3,163; mean 50.52; 95% CI 48.16 – 52.87; 90% 48.54 – 52.49; 99% 47.42 – 53.61. *(tolerance: ± 0.1)*

        **Model answer:** We estimate the average basket at 50.52, and values between 48.16 and 52.87 are compatible with the data (95%). Higher confidence widens the interval.

        **Common wrong answer:** Computing on lines instead of baskets, or saying '95% of baskets are between 48 and 53'.

    **Leader drill:** How would you word this in the annual report for non-specialists?

??? example "R3-2 · Applied · Does the t-interval deserve trust here?"
    **Business framing:** A junior asks whether the textbook interval is reliable for basket value.

    **Deliverable:** Simulate coverage of the 95% t-interval for samples of n = 30 and n = 200 drawn from all baskets (4,000 samples each).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Coverage 84.2% at n = 30 and 88.2% at n = 200 — both below 95%. *(tolerance: ± 1.5 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Bulk buyers make basket value extremely skewed (skew 10.5); most samples miss them, so the interval is too narrow and too low. Use a bootstrap interval, the median, or a log-scale analysis.

        **Common wrong answer:** Assuming n ≥ 30 makes the CLT kick in for any data.

    **Leader drill:** Which KPIs in your organisation are likely to have this problem?

??? example "R3-3 · Stretch · Precision you can buy"
    **Business framing:** Marketing wants the mean basket of a new loyalty tier estimated to within ± 3.

    **Deliverable:** Expected 95% CI width for n = 100, 400 and 1,600 baskets using the chain's SD; the n needed for a half-width of 3.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Widths 26.82, 13.29, 6.63; half-width 3 needs about 1,949 baskets. *(tolerance: ± 5%)*

        **Model answer:** Width falls with √n: four times the data halves it. With SD ≈ 67.58, ± 3 needs n ≈ (1.96 × SD / 3)². And the heavy tail means even that interval under-covers — plan for more or use the median.

        **Common wrong answer:** Scaling width linearly with n.

    **Leader drill:** Is ± 3 the right precision target? How would you decide?

### Finance · Meridian

??? example "F3-1 · Basic · Default rate with a Wilson interval"
    **Business framing:** The CRO wants the portfolio default rate with its uncertainty.

    **Deliverable:** 12-month default rate overall and for students with 95% Wilson intervals.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Overall 4.0% (3.76 – 4.25); students 6.48% (5.29 – 7.9). *(tolerance: ± 0.05 pp)*

        **Model answer:** The student interval lies wholly above the overall rate — students are riskier, and the data are precise enough to say so.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using the Wald interval without checking it, or omitting n.

    **Leader drill:** Would you price students differently on this evidence alone?

??? example "F3-2 · Applied · The branch league table"
    **Business framing:** The network director ranks branches by default rate and wants to reward the best.

    **Deliverable:** Default rate by branch with Wilson CIs and n; number of branches whose interval is wider than 2 pp; the lowest-rate branch.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Rates range 0.52% – 5.54%; 14 of 20 intervals are wider than 2 pp; lowest BR18 (8 / 562, CI 0.72 – 2.78%). *(tolerance: ± 0.05 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Many intervals overlap; the 'best' branch is the smallest, in the low-default region R4. A ranking without intervals and without adjusting for region mix rewards luck and geography.

        **Common wrong answer:** Ranking on point estimates.

    **Leader drill:** What would a fair branch scorecard look like?

??? example "F3-3 · Stretch · Wald vs Wilson at fraud rates"
    **Business framing:** The fraud team reports monthly fraud rates from samples of 300 accounts with Wald intervals.

    **Deliverable:** Simulate coverage of 95% Wald and Wilson intervals at the fraud base rate, n = 300.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Wald 88.1%, Wilson 94.5%; 11.8% of samples contain zero frauds. *(tolerance: ± 1.5 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Wald collapses to 0–0 when no fraud is sampled and under-covers badly for rare events. Use Wilson or Clopper-Pearson.

        **Common wrong answer:** Reporting '0% fraud (95% CI 0–0%)'.

    **Leader drill:** What sample size would you require for monthly fraud-rate reporting?

## 10. Recap

Estimate + interval + n + method. Width ∝ 1/√n. Wilson for rates; bootstrap for skew; independent units only.
