=== "Finance"

## 2. Concept

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    ### 2.1 The question every two-group test answers

    *"Is the difference I see between group A and group B larger than the difference I would expect from chance alone — and how
    big is it?"*

    Three ingredients decide the answer:

    | Ingredient | Bigger means… | Retail example |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Signal** — the observed difference | easier to detect | test stores grew more than control |
    | **Noise** — how much units vary | harder to detect | stores differ hugely in size |
    | **Sample size** — how many independent units | easier to detect | 20 stores per arm |

    A test statistic is simply **signal ÷ noise**, where noise shrinks as sample size grows.

    ### 2.2 Step 1 — what is the unit of analysis?

    **The unit of analysis is whatever was randomised (or sampled independently).** NorthStar randomised *stores*, so each store
    contributes **one** number. Treating 320 store-weeks or thousands of transaction lines as independent pretends you have far
    more information than you do — the p-value becomes too small and the interval too narrow.

    ### 2.3 Step 2 — what type is the outcome?

    | Outcome | Example | Summary per group | Test |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Continuous** | units, revenue, growth index | mean (and median) | **Welch's t-test** |
    | **Binary** | defaulted yes/no | proportion (rate) | **Two-proportion z-test** (Fisher's exact if counts are small) |

    ### 2.4 Welch's t-test (continuous outcome)

    **In plain English:** *t = (difference in group means) ÷ (standard error of that difference).* The standard error combines
    each group's spread divided by its size, so groups may have **different** variances (that is what makes it Welch's version —
    the safer default).

    \[ t = \frac{\bar x_A - \bar x_B}{\sqrt{s_A^2/n_A + s_B^2/n_B}} \]

    A large |t| means the difference is many standard errors away from zero. The p-value is the probability of a difference at
    least this large **if** the true difference were zero. The 95% **confidence interval** is roughly the difference ± 2 standard
    errors: the range of true effects compatible with the data.

    ### 2.5 Making stores comparable — growth versus their own baseline

    Hypermarkets sell many times more Hair Care than express stores. If, by chance, a few more big stores land in one arm, a
    simple comparison of weekly averages is swamped by size differences. The fix used here: for each store, divide its average in
    the test window (weeks 70–77) by **its own** average in the 16 weeks before (54–69). This **growth index** removes size; the
    test then compares the average growth index between arms. (Day 4 generalises this idea as CUPED.)

    ### 2.6 Two-proportion z-test (binary outcome)

    **In plain English:** *z = (difference in rates) ÷ (standard error of the difference under "no difference").* With large
    counts this is reliable; when any expected cell count is below about 5, use **Fisher's exact test** instead.

    ### 2.7 When the data are heavy-tailed or small

    If the outcome is very skewed (basket values!) **and** groups are small, a few extreme values can dominate the mean. Then use a
    **Mann-Whitney U test** (compares ranks: "does a random unit from A tend to exceed one from B?") or a **bootstrap CI**. With
    large samples, the t-test on means stays valid thanks to the central limit theorem — but always report the median too.

    ### 2.8 Effect size and practical significance

    | Measure | Use for | Meaning |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Difference and **% lift** with CI | continuous | how much more, in business units |
    | **Cohen's d / Hedges' g** | continuous | difference in standard deviations (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large — Cohen's rough benchmarks) |
    | **Risk difference** (pp) with CI | binary | extra events per 100 |
    | **Relative risk**, **odds ratio** | binary | ratio of rates / odds |

    **Statistically significant ≠ important.** With huge samples tiny effects become "significant"; with small samples important
    effects can be "not significant". Decide on the **confidence interval versus the smallest effect worth acting on**.

=== "Leader"

    !!! abstract "Refresh in five minutes"
        - Two-group test = *signal ÷ noise × √n*. Unit of analysis = unit of randomisation.
        - Continuous → **Welch t** (default; tolerates unequal variances). Binary → **two-proportion z** (Fisher if expected cells < 5).
        - Heavy tails + small n → Mann-Whitney / bootstrap. Large n → t on means is fine; still show medians.
        - Report **effect size + CI**; judge against the **minimum effect worth acting on**, not against p < 0.05.
        - Experiments: check **randomisation and sample ratio** before the result.

    **Where judgement is needed** — the five questions that decide whether a two-group result can be trusted:

    1. **What was randomised, and is that the unit analysed?** (stores vs lines vs store-weeks)
    2. **Were the groups comparable before the treatment?** (balance check, sample ratio)
    3. **Is the outcome defined identically for both groups and over the same window?**
    4. **How big is the effect, and how uncertain?** (CI against the business threshold)
    5. **What else could produce this difference?** (confounding, seasonality, mix, novelty, data errors)

    **Where practitioners genuinely disagree** (know the positions; pick one and be consistent):

    - *Welch by default vs Student's t.* Many methodologists recommend Welch always (it loses little when variances are equal).
      This course follows that view.
    - *Normality tests before t-tests.* Often discouraged: at large n they reject trivially, at small n they lack power. Look at the plot.
    - *α = 0.05.* A convention, not a law. Some propose 0.005 for new claims; regulated settings may pre-specify their own.
    - *One-sided tests.* Defensible only if pre-registered and a result in the other direction would genuinely lead to the same decision.
    - *Frequentist vs Bayesian readouts.* Bayesian "probability the promotion lifts sales by ≥ 5%" is often easier to act on (L4.3).

## 3. When to use, when not, assumptions, fallbacks

| | Continuous (Welch t) | Binary (two-proportion z) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Use when** | comparing average amounts between two independent groups | comparing a rate between two independent groups |
| **Do not use when** | units are paired (→ paired t), clustered and not aggregated (→ aggregate / mixed model), > 2 groups (→ ANOVA), groups not randomised and causal claim needed (→ causal methods) | counts tiny (→ Fisher), paired yes/no (→ McNemar), covariates matter (→ logistic regression) |
| **Assumptions** | independent units; means approximately normal (n ≥ ~30 per group *or* not heavy-tailed) | independent units; expected cells ≥ 5; same definition and window |
| **Diagnostics** | histogram / ECDF and Q-Q per group, skewness, Levene (informative only), outliers | 2×2 table with expected counts; rates by segment |
| **Fallback if a check fails** | Mann-Whitney, bootstrap CI, permutation test, log or growth transform | Fisher's exact; logistic regression; stratified (Mantel-Haenszel) analysis |
| **Recipe** | `rcp_two_group_test` (auto-detects outcome type) | same |

## 5. Output anatomy

=== "Retail"

    Output of `retail_promo_lift.yaml` (abridged from `narrative.md`):

    ```text
    Unit of analysis: store_id · n: test = 20, control = 20 · Outcome: continuous · Primary test: welch t (two-sided, α = 0.05)
    Mean units growth index (post ÷ pre): 1.099 (test) vs 0.967 (control) … +13.6% (bootstrap 95% CI …)
    p = 0.0016; Hedges' g = 1.06
    Robustness: mann whitney p = 0.0009 · permutation p ≈ 0.0019
    ```

    | Number | What it means | Typical range | Good / bad / suspicious |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | n = 20 / 20 | Independent units per arm | Store tests: often 10–50 per arm | Suspicious if n is in the thousands for a store test (wrong unit) |
    | Growth index 1.099 vs 0.967 | Window average ÷ own baseline | ~0.8–1.2 for a stable category | Control below 1 here reflects seasonality — that is why a control group is needed |
    | Lift +13.6% | Relative difference in growth | In-store feature promos: low single digits to ~20% (rule of thumb; varies widely by category) | A lift > 50% from signage alone would be suspicious: check for data errors or a confounded rollout |
    | 95% CI 5.5% to 21.7% | Range of true lifts compatible with the data | Width shrinks with √n | Wide here: only 40 stores |
    | p = 0.0016 | Chance of a difference this large if the true lift were 0 | — | Not the probability the promotion works; not the size of the effect |
    | Hedges' g = 1.06 | Difference in SD units (small-sample corrected) | 0.2 / 0.5 / 0.8 = small / medium / large (Cohen, rough) | Large g with a wide CI is common in small tests |
    | Shapiro p (min) = 0.002, max \|skew\| = 1.84 | Shape of the growth index | — | Below the \|2\| rule of thumb; with heavier skew and n < 30 switch to Mann-Whitney / bootstrap — robustness tests agree here |
    | MDE (80% power) ≈ 11.2% | Smallest lift this design reliably detects | — | The test cannot reliably confirm effects much smaller than the MDE |

    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Rates 6.47% / 3.35% | 12-month default rate per group | Unsecured retail books: low-to-high single digits (rule of thumb; varies by product, cycle, cut-off) | Rates > 20% in a prime book would be suspicious (definition or data error) |
    | Risk difference +3.12 pp | Extra defaults per 100 accounts | — | The decision-relevant number: ~3.1 extra defaults per 100 |

## 6. Scenario matrix

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    | Result pattern | Interpretation | Next check |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Significant, CI entirely above the threshold | Effect is real and large enough | Guardrails (margin, returns), then roll out |
    | Significant, CI straddles the threshold | Effect is real; may be too small to pay | Extend test or staged rollout |
    | Not significant, CI wide (includes 0 and the threshold) | **Inconclusive** — test too small | Power / MDE; bigger or longer test |
    | Not significant, CI narrow around 0 | Evidence of no meaningful effect | Stop; record the learning |
    | t-test and Mann-Whitney disagree | Tails or outliers drive the mean | Inspect extremes; report both |
    | Huge n, tiny effect, p ≈ 0 | Statistically, not practically, significant | Judge on effect size |

=== "Leader"

    | Result pattern | Interpretation | Likely causes | Next checks | Possible actions — why / when / when not | Norms (rules of thumb) |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Significant; lower CI ≥ threshold** | Real and worthwhile | Genuine effect | Guardrails; novelty (does lift decay week by week?); format/segment consistency | **Roll out** — when guardrails hold and the test resembles rollout conditions. *Not* when the test period was unusual (holiday) | α 0.05 two-sided, power 80% (conventions) |
    | **Significant; CI straddles threshold** (this retail test) | Real, size uncertain | Small test | MDE vs threshold; cost of a wrong rollout vs delay | **Staged rollout with a holdout** — when the downside is reversible. **Extend test** — when rollout is costly/irreversible. *Not* full rollout on the point estimate | Many trade promotions fail to break even in industry studies — size matters more than significance |
    | **Not significant; wide CI** | Inconclusive | Under-powered (MDE ≫ threshold) | Power calculation; variance reduction | **Redesign** (more stores, longer pre-period, CUPED). *Never* report "no effect" | Ask for MDE before launch |
    | **Not significant; narrow CI around 0** | No meaningful effect | Ineffective treatment | Implementation fidelity (did stores execute?) | **Stop**; reallocate budget | — |
    | **Effect far larger than plausible** | Too good to be true | Wrong unit, data error, confounded rollout, SRM | Row accounting; sample ratio; pre-period balance; outliers | **Hold** until explained | Feature-only promos rarely exceed ~20% (varies) |
    | **Pre-period imbalance** | Arms differed before treatment | Chance with few units; broken randomisation | Randomisation log; stratification | Use growth vs baseline / ANCOVA; re-randomise future tests | Stratify by format/region |
    | **Large overall gap, smaller within segments** (finance) | Confounding by mix | Channel, vintage, product mix | Stratify; regression with covariates | **Do not** set policy on the raw gap; estimate the adjusted effect | Always stratify by channel and vintage in credit |
    | **Tiny effect, p ≈ 0, huge n** | Not practically important | Large portfolio | Effect per 1,000; cost/benefit | Act only if the economics justify it | Translate to money |

    !!! question "Questions to ask the junior"
        - "What was randomised, and how many independent units do you have?"
        - "Show me the pre-period balance and the sample ratio."
        - "What is the CI, and what is the smallest effect that would change our decision?"
        - "What else could explain this — season, mix, a parallel campaign, a data change?"
        - "If you had found the opposite result, what would you have recommended?"

    !!! danger "Red flags in a junior's two-group analysis"
        n in the thousands for a store test · p-value without CI · "no difference" from a non-significant result · t-test on a 0/1
        outcome without proportion language · one-sided test chosen after seeing the data · segments sliced until one is significant ·
        outcome window differs between groups · post-outcome fields in the comparison.

## 7. Alternatives and experiments to try

!!! tip "What else could I try?"
    - **[Permutation test](../../navigator/#N-TEST-PERM)** — Small samples or unusual statistics; minimal assumptions.
    - **[Bayesian A/B test (Beta-Binomial; P(B > A), expected loss)](../../navigator/#N-BAYES-AB)** — Decision-makers want probability statements; prior knowledge exists.
    - **[Regression of outcome on group + pre-period covariate (ANCOVA / CUPED-style)](../../navigator/#N-REG-ANCOVA)** — Two-group comparison with a strong baseline covariate.
    - **[CUPED / pre-period covariate adjustment](../../navigator/#N-EXP-CUPED)** — High-variance metrics with a correlated pre-period measure.
    - **[Stratified analysis — check for Simpson's paradox and confounding](../../navigator/#N-DESC-STRATIFY)** — An overall comparison may be distorted by a third variable (format, channel, vintage).

    **Business framing:** Are loyalty baskets more likely to contain at least one promoted item?

    **Deliverable:** Two-proportion test on baskets; risk difference with CI.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** 51.9% vs 46.3%, p = 0.0017, difference CI 2.1 to 9.1 pp. *(tolerance: ± 0.5 pp)*

        **Model answer:** The binary outcome needs a proportion test, not a t-test on 0/1. The effect is small; whether it matters depends on margin, not on p.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using a t-test on basket counts of promo items.

    **Leader drill:** Is a difference of this size meaningful for promotional planning? What else would you want to know?


??? example "R-A5 · Applied · How much do data errors move the answer?"
    **Business framing:** Twenty-five lines have unit prices 100× the list price.

    **Deliverable:** Mean basket value with and without those lines.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** 55.53 with errors vs 50.52 without. *(tolerance: ± 0.5)*

        **Model answer:** A handful of decimal-shift errors moves the mean basket by several percent. Means are fragile; always clean known errors (and report it) before testing.

        **Common wrong answer:** Silently deleting the rows without reporting, or leaving them in.

    **Leader drill:** Write the one-line data note you expect in the appendix of any deck using basket values.


??? example "R-S1 · Stretch · A null result done properly — weekend vs weekday baskets"
    **Business framing:** Store ops believe Friday–Saturday baskets are bigger.

    **Deliverable:** Welch, Mann-Whitney, and a bootstrap CI of the difference in medians.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Means 49.98 vs 50.54, difference CI -5.76 to 4.63 (p = 0.8308); median difference -1.25 (bootstrap CI -4.56 to 0.95). *(tolerance: ± 0.5)*

        **Model answer:** No evidence that weekend baskets are bigger — weekends have *more* baskets, not bigger ones. The CI shows which differences are ruled out, which is more useful than 'not significant'.

        **Common wrong answer:** 'There is no difference' (absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence) without quoting the interval.

    **Leader drill:** How do you present a null result so it still informs staffing decisions?


??? example "R-S2 · Stretch · Permutation test"
    **Business framing:** Confirm the promo-lift p-value without distributional assumptions.

    **Deliverable:** Permutation test on the 40 store growth indices (10,000 shuffles, seeded).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** p ≈ 0.0019 *(tolerance: ± 0.005)*

        **Model answer:** Shuffling arm labels among the 40 stores mimics the randomisation itself, so the permutation p-value is exact under H0. It agrees with Welch.

        **Common wrong answer:** Shuffling transaction lines or store-weeks.

    **Leader drill:** When would you insist on a permutation test rather than a t-test?


??? example "R-S3 · Stretch · Was the test big enough?"
    **Business framing:** Plan the next store test.

    **Deliverable:** Minimum detectable effect with 20 stores per arm at 80% power; stores per arm needed to detect the 10% threshold.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** MDE ≈ 11.2%; about 25 stores per arm needed for a 10% lift (growth-index SD ≈ 0.126). *(tolerance: MDE ± 0.5 pp; stores ± 5)*

        **Model answer:** The test was powered for effects of about 11.2%. To confirm the 10% threshold reliably you need more stores, a longer pre-period, or variance reduction (Day 4, CUPED).

        **Common wrong answer:** Using post-hoc 'observed power'.

    **Leader drill:** The business can offer 30 stores per arm. What do you recommend?


??? example "R-S4 · Stretch · Does the lift differ by format?"
    **Business framing:** A format manager points to the format-level lifts and wants their format treated differently in the rollout.

    **Deliverable:** Lift by format with arm counts; your judgement.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Hyper 22.0% (4 v 4 stores), Super 11.1%, Express 12.3%. *(tolerance: ± 0.5 pp)*

        **Model answer:** With only 4 stores per arm in Hyper (and 7–9 elsewhere), format estimates are extremely noisy. This is a hypothesis for a future test, not a finding. Subgroup slicing after the fact invites false discoveries (L3.7).

        **Common wrong answer:** Changing the rollout for a format on the basis of 8–18 stores.

    **Leader drill:** How do you respond to the format manager without dismissing them?


??? example "R-S5 · Stretch · Preview — the e-coupon A/B test"
    **Business framing:** The digital team ran a 50/50 e-coupon test and wants a two-proportion test.

    **Deliverable:** Conversion by arm, two-proportion p-value, and the user counts per arm.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** 11.5% vs 11.07%, p = 0.466; but users 6,000 vs 5,421 — far from 50/50. *(tolerance: ± 0.1 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Before testing, check the sample ratio. A 50/50 design producing this split is a red flag (L4.1, SRM). Do not interpret the conversion difference until the cause is found.

        **Common wrong answer:** Reporting 'no significant lift' and closing the test.

    **Leader drill:** Do you allow the team to report this test? What must happen first?

??? example "F-F1 · Basic · Default rate by income verification"
    **Business framing:** Credit policy asks whether customers with unverified income default more.

    **Deliverable:** Accounts, defaults and 12-month default rate for verified and unverified.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Unverified 336/5,194 = 6.47%; verified 664/19,806 = 3.35%. *(tolerance: ± 0.05 pp)*

        **Common wrong answer:** Reporting rates without counts.

    **Leader drill:** What is the first question you ask about how 'default' was defined?


??? example "F-F2 · Basic · Two-proportion test"
    **Business framing:** Is the difference larger than chance?

    **Deliverable:** Run `finance_default_by_verification.yaml`. Risk difference with 95% CI, z and p.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** +3.12 pp (95% CI 2.4 to 3.83), z = 10.2, p ≈ 1.95e-24. *(tolerance: ± 0.05 pp)*

        **Model answer:** The difference is far beyond chance. Expected cell counts are large, so the z-test is appropriate (Fisher would agree).

        **Common wrong answer:** Running a t-test on the 0/1 default flag and quoting a 'mean difference' without a proportion interpretation.

    **Leader drill:** Translate the result into 'per 1,000 accounts'.


??? example "F-F3 · Basic · Relative risk vs odds ratio"
    **Business framing:** The risk committee wants one number.

    **Deliverable:** Relative risk and odds ratio; explain the difference.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** RR = 1.93, OR = 1.99. *(tolerance: ± 0.02)*

        **Model answer:** Relative risk is the ratio of default rates and is easiest to explain. The odds ratio is close to it here because defaults are rare; the two diverge when outcomes are common.

        **Common wrong answer:** Saying the odds ratio means 'unverified customers are 1.9 times as likely to default' when outcomes are common.

    **Leader drill:** Which do you put in the committee paper and why?


??? example "F-F4 · Basic · Per 100 customers"
    **Business framing:** Explain the effect to a branch manager.

    **Deliverable:** Extra defaults per 100 unverified customers.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** About 3.1 extra defaults per 100 unverified customers. *(tolerance: ± 0.1)*

        **Model answer:** Natural frequencies are understood far better than percentages or odds.

        **Common wrong answer:** Saying 'unverified customers are 84% more likely to default' to a non-technical audience without the base rate.

    **Leader drill:** Write the one sentence for the branch manager.


??? example "F-F5 · Basic · A continuous comparison"
    **Business framing:** How different are defaulters' bureau scores?

    **Deliverable:** Mean bureau score of defaulters vs non-defaulters; Welch CI; Cohen's d.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** 637.1 vs 699.4, difference -62.3 (CI -65.1 to -59.5), d = -1.05. *(tolerance: ± 0.5)*

        **Model answer:** A large standardised difference — yet the distributions still overlap heavily (probability a random non-defaulter outscores a random defaulter ≈ 0.801). A large d does not mean the score alone separates goods from bads.

        **Common wrong answer:** Concluding that bureau score 'predicts default perfectly'.

    **Leader drill:** Why is a d of this size compatible with a model AUC of only ~0.8?


??? example "F-A1 · Applied · Acquisition channel"
    **Business framing:** Default by channel.

    **Deliverable:** Default rate and counts by acquisition channel.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Branch 2.86%, online 2.83%, partner 13.49% (n = 2,714). *(tolerance: ± 0.05 pp)*

        **Model answer:** The partner channel defaults several times more often. Since there are three groups, a chi-square test (L3.5) is the formal test; here the gap is obvious.

        **Common wrong answer:** Running three separate pairwise tests without correction.

    **Leader drill:** What would you ask about the partner channel before blaming it?


??? example "F-A2 · Applied · What else could explain the verification effect?"
    **Business framing:** Unverified income is far more common in the partner channel.

    **Deliverable:** Share unverified by channel; default by verification within each channel.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Unverified share: partner 45.8% vs others 17.7%. Within channel — branch 3.93% vs 2.63%, online 3.65% vs 2.65%, partner 14.96% vs 12.24%. *(tolerance: ± 0.1 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Part of the overall 3.12 pp gap is channel mix (confounding). Within channels the gap is smaller but still positive. The overall comparison overstates the verification effect; a regression with channel (Day 5) estimates it properly.

        **Common wrong answer:** Quoting the overall 3.12 pp as the causal effect of verification.

    **Leader drill:** Credit policy wants to require verification for all applicants based on the 3.12 pp. What do you tell them?


??? example "F-A3 · Applied · Skewed outcome — utilisation"
    **Business framing:** Do defaulters have higher card utilisation?

    **Deliverable:** Median utilisation by default status; Mann-Whitney and Welch p-values.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Medians 0.549 vs 0.442; Mann-Whitney p ≈ 1.29e-27; Welch p ≈ 2.12e-27. *(tolerance: ± 0.005)*

        **Model answer:** Both tests agree with large samples. The relationship is non-linear (risk jumps above ~80% utilisation, L5.6), which a single mean difference hides.

        **Common wrong answer:** Concluding a linear relationship from a difference in medians.

    **Leader drill:** How would you present utilisation risk to the policy team?


??? example "F-A4 · Applied · Champion vs challenger (preview of L4.5)"
    **Business framing:** Collections tested a new strategy on 20% of accounts entering 30 DPD.

    **Deliverable:** Run `finance_challenger_cure.yaml`; cure-rate difference with CI.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** +6.0 pp (95% CI 2.4 to 9.7), n = 860 challenger vs 3,335 champion. *(tolerance: ± 0.2 pp)*

        **Model answer:** Same recipe, different dataset, config change only. The challenger cures more accounts; the 80/20 split makes the challenger estimate the less precise one.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using `strategy_group` or `cured_within_3m` as predictors in a default model (they are known only after delinquency).

    **Leader drill:** The CI lower bound is about 2.4 pp. Is that enough to switch strategy? What guardrails do you check?


??? example "F-A5 · Applied · Small counts — Fisher's exact test"
    **Business framing:** Is the default rate different for the top-1% income customers?

    **Deliverable:** Defaults and rate in the top 1% by income; Fisher's exact p-value; expected defaults if the rate were average.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** 11 defaults among 241 (4.56%), expected ≈ 8.5; Fisher p = 0.3753. *(tolerance: ± 0.02)*

        **Model answer:** With single-digit counts, use Fisher's exact test. There is no evidence of a difference in either direction — and too few events to rule out a meaningful one.

        **Common wrong answer:** Using the z-test with very small counts, or concluding anything about high earners from a handful of defaults.

    **Leader drill:** Would you approve a special limit policy for this group based on this analysis?


??? example "F-S1 · Stretch · A median that is zero"
    **Business framing:** Compare current balances of defaulters and non-defaulters with a bootstrap CI of the median difference.

    **Deliverable:** Medians, bootstrap CI, and an explanation of anything surprising.

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** Defaulter median balance = 0, non-defaulter 6,899 (CI of difference -6,995 to -6,820). *(tolerance: ± 50)*

        **Model answer:** The defaulter median is zero because charged-off balances are written off — `balance_current` is measured *after* the outcome. It is a timing (leakage) trap, not a finding about defaulters.

        **Common wrong answer:** Concluding defaulters had low balances.

    **Leader drill:** What rule would you add to the team's checklist after seeing this?


??? example "F-S2 · Stretch · Sample size for a small difference"
    **Business framing:** Risk wants to detect a 0.5 pp difference around a 4% default rate.

    **Deliverable:** Accounts needed per group (α = 0.05 two-sided, 80% power).

    ??? success "Expected finding and model answer"
        **Expected:** About 24,112 accounts per group. *(tolerance: ± 500)*

        **Model answer:** Small differences on rare outcomes need very large samples — a key reason credit tests run for many months or use proxies (early delinquency).

        **Common wrong answer:** Planning a test with 2,000 accounts per arm.

    **Leader drill:** How do you shorten the test without losing validity?

## 11. Coaching cards and recap

=== "Basic → Advanced"

    **Recap**

    - Analyse at the unit that was randomised.
    - Continuous → Welch t; binary → two-proportion z (Fisher if small); heavy tails + small n → Mann-Whitney / bootstrap.
    - Always report effect size **with a CI**; compare it with the smallest effect worth acting on.
    - Non-significant ≠ no effect. Significant ≠ important.
    - Before believing a difference, ask what else could explain it.

=== "Leader"

    !!! tip "Coaching card 1 — interpreting the number"
        Ask the junior to say the result in one sentence **without** the word "significant". Example: *"Test stores grew about 13.6% more
        than control; the true lift could plausibly be anywhere from 5.5% to 21.7%."* If they cannot, they do not yet own the result.

    !!! tip "Coaching card 2 — business interpretation"
        Make them put the CI next to the business threshold on a single line chart. The decision becomes visible: roll out, stage,
        extend or stop. Then ask what the cost of each wrong decision is.

    !!! tip "Coaching card 3 — the confounding reflex"
        For any observational comparison (verification, loyalty), require a stratified table by the two most likely confounders
        before any recommendation. In credit: channel and vintage. In retail: format and region.
